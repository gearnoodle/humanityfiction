# HF010: Who cleans the drains?

> Published on Nov 1, 2026

Every old-worlder asks this eventually, usually with a slightly smug face.

"Fine. Nobody has to do anything. So who cleans the drains?"

On Tuesday, the drain on Library Street blocked. The Earth-monitor said:

> **Drain, Library Street** · Status: blocked · Smell: noticeable

I decided to watch what happened.

By noon, three had turned up:

- **Raili,** a retired engineer who thinks sewers are the most beautiful thing humans ever built. "Have you seen the brickwork down there? 1890s. Gorgeous."
- **Jonne,** sixteen, who films the grossest jobs in town and has a surprising number of fans.
- **A small robot,** built from an open design, which went into the pipe first so nobody else had to. Its name is Kevin.

It took forty minutes. Afterwards, someone had heated the sauna for them. Nobody arranged that.

## The old answer

In the old world, the answer to "who cleans the drains?" was usually: whoever had the fewest other options. The dirtiest and most dangerous jobs went to the people who couldn't say no, and they were often paid the least.

That's not an answer. That's the problem.

## The new answer

There isn't one answer. There are several, stacked on top of each other.

**Machines take the worst part.** Robots go into the pipes first. If a job is so bad that nobody would ever choose it, that's not a people problem. It's a design problem.

**Things are built to be easy to clean.** New drains open up like a lunchbox. The one on Library Street is old, which is why it keeps blocking.

**Nobody does it forever.** An unpleasant afternoon is fine. Forty unpleasant years isn't. Jobs get cut small enough that anyone can take one bite.

**People are stranger than we think.** Almost nothing is so disgusting that nobody, anywhere, finds it fascinating. There are drain historians, pipe hobbyists, and kids like Jonne. The old world just never asked them.

**The people most bothered fix things first.** In the rare case that nobody comes, the smell gets worse, and sooner or later someone on that street decides they've had enough. That isn't the system failing. Being motivated by your own nose is perfectly fine.

**Real danger is an emergency.** If a blocked drain ever threatens people's health, it stops being a hobby question. Emergencies bring many hands, quickly.

## Backups for the backups

Underneath all of that, there's slack. Much more than seems sensible.

Every job on the Earth-monitor has a sign-up list. Signing up isn't a promise. It just says: "I might do this."

- **First in line:** the people who'll go when it happens. For the Library Street drain, that was Raili.
- **Waitlist:** if the first ones can't make it. Jonne was here.
- **The waitlist for the waitlist:** "if those people can't either, try me."
- **The lightest queue:** "only if literally nobody else turns up." Hundreds of people sit on these lists for almost everything. It costs nothing, and almost nobody ever gets called. Kevin is on all of them.

When a job has nobody signed up at all, it starts climbing up people's pages, a little higher every hour. And then something funny happens: people race for it. An empty list is rare enough to be exciting. Being the first name on one is a small thrill, like finding fresh snow nobody has walked on yet.

Stack all of that together, and it becomes statistically impossible for a job to go undone. Not because anyone has to do it. Because so many people might.

That's also what makes stopping safe. With forty people quietly standing behind you, you can step out of line any time.

## What really changed

The work didn't get any less dirty. What changed is who does it, and why.

In the old world, the drain person was invisible, and often stuck. Here, it's a retired engineer having the time of her life, a teenager with fans, and a robot called Kevin.

* * *

I joined them on Thursday, when the drain blocked again. It was exactly as disgusting as I'd expected.

The sauna was excellent.
