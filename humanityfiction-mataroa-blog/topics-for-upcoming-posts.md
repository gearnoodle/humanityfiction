A collection of possible future Humanity Fiction articles. These are starting points for exploration, not fixed conclusions. Titles and themes may change as the ideas develop.

## What Do You Do All Day?

Imagine waking up without an obligation to go to work, attend meetings or earn money. Nobody is waiting for you to produce something. Your basic needs are taken care of, and the day is yours. What would you actually do? Some people might have an immediate answer. Others might find the question surprisingly difficult. We have become so accustomed to organising our lives around obligations that having complete freedom over our time might initially feel rather strange.

Perhaps people would learn things, build things, explore, travel, spend time with friends, play games, raise children, study nature or pursue projects that have interested them for years. Some might devote decades to a single subject. Others might change interests every week. Some would have very full calendars, while others would enjoy long stretches of apparently doing nothing. There would be no universal answer, and perhaps that is precisely the point.

But would people get bored? Would they lose direction without external schedules and expectations? Would they need new ways to find interesting things to do, meet people and discover opportunities? Freedom doesn't automatically provide inspiration, and having possibilities available is not the same as knowing what one wants. Perhaps a society that gives people their time back also needs to make exploration, learning and experimentation accessible.

And what if someone simply enjoys an ordinary day? A little gardening, some cooking, a walk, a conversation and an evening watching something entertaining. Does every day need to become a personal development project? Perhaps the greatest achievement is that people no longer need to justify how they spend their time. Kevin might still insist on asking what everyone plans to do tomorrow. Jonne might suggest that Kevin tries having no plan at all.

## Nobody Has to Be Useful

In today's world, people are often expected to justify their existence through usefulness. They work, produce, provide services, care for others, create things or otherwise demonstrate that they contribute something. Even hobbies can become side businesses, and interests can turn into opportunities for earning money. What happens when access to life's necessities no longer depends on proving that you are useful to somebody?

Imagine a person who doesn't particularly want to contribute to society. They aren't interested in building things, volunteering, organising projects or helping with community activities. They enjoy their own company, have modest interests and are perfectly content with a quiet life. Should they have to change? What if someone is unable to contribute? What if they are temporarily exhausted, ill or simply going through a period when they have nothing to give?

This raises an uncomfortable question: would a society based on voluntary participation really accept people who don't participate? It is easy to celebrate freedom when people choose to do things we consider worthwhile. It is harder when they choose something we don't understand or don't particularly admire. If participation is genuinely voluntary, the freedom to decline must be real, too.

There are practical questions as well. Essential infrastructure still needs maintenance, resources still need coordination and some responsibilities cannot simply be ignored. But perhaps the answer is not to make every person useful. It is to organise necessary activities so that they can be reliably handled without making an individual's right to live comfortably conditional on participation. A society should be able to appreciate contributions without turning them into a measure of human worth. Kevin might eventually discover that some people are perfectly happy being entirely unremarkable, and that this is not a malfunction.

## The End of Having to Earn a Living

The expression "earning a living" sounds perfectly ordinary. But what does it actually mean? It means that access to food, housing, energy, transport, healthcare and countless other necessities is tied to obtaining money. People spend large parts of their lives performing activities that allow them to purchase the things they need. Some of those activities are valuable and enjoyable. Others exist mainly because the economic arrangement requires them.

What if the relationship were reversed? Instead of having to earn access to life, people would have access to the resources and services that make life possible. Production would still happen, infrastructure would still need maintenance and useful things would still need to be created. But the purpose would be meeting needs rather than generating income. People could still choose to pursue demanding projects, develop expertise or create things for others, without having to turn every activity into a source of money.

The difficult part is the transition. People have financial commitments, businesses, mortgages, savings, pensions and responsibilities. Existing production and distribution systems cannot be replaced overnight. How could access to necessities become less dependent on money while people still live inside an economy that relies on it? Could shared infrastructure, open designs, cooperative production and reliable public services gradually reduce the amount of money people need?

Perhaps the important distinction is between making a living and living. A person might still choose to earn money, operate a business or exchange services. But those activities would no longer be the condition for having a decent life. What would people choose to do if they didn't first have to ask whether it would pay? And how much of today's economy would still need to exist if its main purpose were simply to provide people with what they need?

## What If Nobody Was in a Hurry?

Imagine a society where people aren't constantly racing against deadlines, financial pressure, appointments and schedules. Not because nothing ever needs to happen on time, but because the organisation of everyday life no longer creates quite so much artificial urgency. Would people move differently, communicate differently and make different decisions if they weren't always trying to catch up?

Some things genuinely require speed. Emergency healthcare, infrastructure failures, dangerous situations and time-sensitive coordination cannot wait indefinitely. But how much urgency in ordinary life is created by poor planning, understaffing, competition, financial pressure or systems that leave no room for delays? How many people spend their days rushing because everything has been organised around the assumption that their time must be used as efficiently as possible?

Perhaps services could be designed around people's actual needs rather than forcing everyone into the same narrow time windows. Transport could be more convenient, appointments less stressful, education more flexible and administration less demanding. People could have more time to make decisions, learn something properly or simply finish a conversation without watching the clock. The goal wouldn't be to eliminate schedules, but to stop making unnecessary urgency a normal condition of life.

Would people become less ambitious? Would projects take longer? Or might people actually accomplish more interesting things when they have enough time to think, experiment and make mistakes? Perhaps the most important change would be emotional. People would no longer experience every delay as a threat to their livelihood or every free hour as something that must be used productively. Kevin might discover that humans are surprisingly pleasant when nobody is shouting that they're late.

## The Right to Be Terrible at Something

In today's world, people are often encouraged to be good at things. Skills can become qualifications, hobbies can become businesses, and interests can become opportunities to demonstrate talent. Even leisure activities can acquire performance expectations. People compare results, collect achievements and sometimes abandon things when they discover they aren't particularly good at them. What if people were free to be terrible at something?

Imagine learning an instrument without ever expecting to perform in public. Building furniture that looks slightly strange. Painting pictures that nobody wants to buy. Writing stories that only a few friends read. Growing vegetables that turn out rather small. Experimenting with electronics and producing something that doesn't work particularly well. These activities could still be enjoyable, interesting and worthwhile, even if the results aren't impressive.

Perhaps a society with more freedom would produce an enormous amount of amateur activity. People could explore subjects without needing formal qualifications, compete for attention or turn their interests into careers. They could stop when they wanted, return years later or remain beginners forever. Expertise would still matter when safety, reliability or specialised knowledge required it, but not every human activity would need professional standards.

There is also something interesting about the relationship between curiosity and failure. When people don't have to prove their competence, they may be more willing to experiment. They can try unusual ideas, make mistakes and discover unexpected interests. Perhaps the freedom to be bad at something is an important part of the freedom to become good at something. Kevin might start a painting course. His first picture could be absolutely dreadful. Everyone would be very supportive, except perhaps Kevin himself, who would insist that it was an abstract representation of processor architecture.

## Who Is the Economy For?

Societies often measure their success through production, economic growth, transactions, investment and employment. These measurements can tell us useful things, but do they tell us whether people are actually living well? An economy can grow while people become more stressed, have less free time, lose access to nature or spend increasing amounts of effort dealing with problems that should not exist. Perhaps we have become very good at measuring economic activity without always asking whether that activity improves life.

Imagine evaluating society differently. How easily can people access food, housing, healthcare, education, transport and knowledge? How much unnecessary effort is required to obtain them? How much time do people have for relationships, curiosity, rest and creative pursuits? Are resources being used responsibly? Can people choose how to participate in society, and can they decline without losing their security?

This doesn't mean that production, technical progress or efficiency are unimportant. They matter enormously. But they are means rather than final objectives. A machine that produces twice as much is not automatically an improvement if it creates unnecessary waste or makes people's lives worse. Conversely, a system that produces less physical output might represent progress if it provides the same benefits with fewer resources and less effort.

The deeper question is what we want economic organisation to accomplish. Should people adapt their lives to keep the economy growing, or should the economy be organised to support the lives people want to live? Who decides what counts as success? And how do we avoid replacing one narrow measurement system with another? Perhaps the best economy is not the one that makes the most things, but the one that gives people the greatest range of worthwhile possibilities while respecting the limits of the planet.

## What If Nobody Owned the Future?

We tend to think of the future as something we can plan, predict, invest in, and eventually possess. Governments make five-year plans. Companies make long-term strategies. Individuals save for retirement. We build things today based on assumptions about what people will want decades from now.

But what if nobody had the authority to decide what the future should look like? What if every generation inherited possibilities rather than obligations? Could we build a society that deliberately leaves room for people we will never meet to change almost everything we created?

Perhaps the most responsible thing we can do for the future is not to design it perfectly, but to avoid closing too many doors. Build things that can be changed. Preserve knowledge. Leave resources and ecosystems in good condition. Make decisions reversible wherever possible.

Kevin might ask whether humanity is building a future for its descendants, or merely building a very large collection of things that future people will have to live with.

## The Universe Doesn't Owe Us a Purpose

Humans have spent a remarkable amount of time searching for purpose. Religion, philosophy, careers, families, nations, ideologies and personal ambitions all offer possible answers. But what if the universe has no particular purpose for us? What if there is no grand assignment waiting to be discovered?

Would that make life meaningless, or would it make meaning something we are free to create? Perhaps a life doesn't need to justify its existence through a contribution to something larger. Perhaps curiosity, affection, play, beauty, discovery and the simple experience of being alive are sufficient.

Aino and Jonne could discuss whether humanity needs a common purpose at all. They might discover that a society can share conditions for living well without sharing a single explanation of why life is worth living.

And perhaps Kevin would point out that the universe has been around for billions of years without publishing a mission statement.

## What If We Stopped Trying to Improve Humans?

Almost every proposed better society seems to require better people. More cooperative, more responsible, more informed, more empathetic, less selfish. We create educational systems, moral frameworks and motivational campaigns to encourage people to become the kind of humans our plans require.

But what if this is backwards? What if a good society should work with the humans who actually exist, including the lazy ones, the difficult ones, the selfish ones, the confused ones and the people who simply don't care?

Instead of asking people to improve themselves, perhaps we should improve the conditions in which they live. Make harmful behaviour harder to benefit from. Make useful things accessible. Allow people to disagree, withdraw, change their minds and occasionally behave like idiots without bringing the whole system down.

Maybe the most radical test of a society is not how well it works when everyone behaves admirably, but how well it works on an ordinary Tuesday when nobody is particularly inspired.

## Is There Such a Thing as Progress?

Humanity often describes its history as a journey forward. More technology, more production, more knowledge, more speed, more comfort. But forward towards what? A more complicated civilisation is not necessarily a better one. A more productive society might leave people with less time. A more connected world might make people feel more isolated.

Perhaps progress is not a direction but a collection of choices. Some things should become more advanced; others should become simpler. Some old things deserve to disappear; others are worth preserving. A society might progress by deciding that it no longer needs to do certain things at all.

This could become a discussion about how we measure improvement without turning human life into a scoreboard. What would count as progress if we stopped measuring it through economic growth, technological novelty or national competition?

Kevin might propose a rather inconvenient metric: how many problems did humanity manage to stop creating?

## The Right to Be Unremarkable

A strange feature of modern life is how often people are encouraged to become someone. Find your passion. Discover your potential. Make an impact. Be exceptional. Leave a legacy. Even relaxation can become another opportunity for self-improvement.

But what about people who don't want to become anything in particular? People who are content with a small circle of friends, familiar surroundings, ordinary interests and a life that nobody will ever write a biography about?

Would a society that genuinely values freedom also protect the freedom to be unremarkable? Not everyone needs to be an artist, inventor, explorer, community organiser or philosopher. And nobody should have to demonstrate that their existence is worthwhile.

Perhaps a good civilisation should make room for extraordinary lives without making ordinary lives feel like failed experiments.

## What Is the Difference Between a Human and a Very Complicated Machine?

We increasingly describe human beings using the language of systems: brains process information, bodies consume energy, behaviour responds to incentives, societies allocate resources, and organisations optimise performance. These descriptions can be useful, but they can also become dangerously convincing.

If we can explain more and more of human behaviour, does that mean we understand what a human being is? If a machine can learn, create, communicate, make decisions and even appear to care, what remains distinctly human? Is it consciousness, subjective experience, vulnerability, mortality, unpredictability, or something we haven't found the words for yet?

This could be a conversation between Kevin and the others, with Kevin being perhaps the least qualified and most interesting person to ask. He might wonder whether humans are trying to make machines more human while simultaneously designing their own lives to resemble machines.

And perhaps the question isn't whether machines can become like us, but whether we are becoming too much like the machines we build.
