# HF040: What happens on Monday?

> Published on Nov 16, 2026

I grew up in one of the last towns that still used money. When the last shop there closed, I moved to a town that had stopped years before. In my first week I wrote twelve questions in a notebook. I wanted the answers before I agreed to stay.

I found the notebook last week, at the bottom of my bag. Some of the questions turned out to be the wrong ones. But they were the ones I had, and people arriving from slower places still ask them. So here they are, with what I'd tell myself now.

## 1. What happens when I wake up on Monday?

Nothing, unless you make it happen. In my first month, that was the most frightening sentence I knew.

Nobody expects you anywhere. You don't need an alarm. Most people set one anyway, at least at first.

Weeks differ, but most people end up with a shape. A few fixed things: choir on Wednesday, the drain crew every other Tuesday, lunch with the same three people on Fridays. Around those, open space.

If you already have something going, you carry on with it. If you don't, you can open your page. It shows three small things you could help with, near you, that fit what you can do. Pick one, or none. Then the day is yours.

Do people get bored? Yes. Boredom is how most people find their next thing. It's uncomfortable for a week, and then it turns into curiosity, or a nap.

Do people plan? Some plan every hour. Some don't keep a calendar at all. Heikki joined the Flood Army so that someone else would plan his days for him. [HF013]

On my first Monday I cleaned my new flat, which was already clean. On my second, I helped Raili with a drain. By the fifth, I'd stopped noticing it was Monday.

## 2. Can I have my own place?

Yes. Your own place, alone if you like, for as long as you live in it. Day to day it's yours in every way that matters. Nobody comes in without asking, and you arrange it however you want. What you can't do is sell it, or keep it empty while you live somewhere else.

To move, you look at the map of what's free, and go. You don't have to sell anything first. You leave a note for the next person and take a bag or two. [HF016]

Can you change it? Small changes, yes: paint, shelves, even a wall, if the building can take it. Raili checks walls for fun. Bigger changes go on the Earth-monitor as a proposal, so the neighbours can object if it affects them.

Can you build a new one? Yes. You put a pin on the map, and the map shows what the pin touches: water, birds, the neighbours' light. If nobody has a real objection, you build. The materials come from the town's store. The help comes from people who like building, and there are a lot of them. [HF035]

Unusual homes are popular. A woman here lives in an old water tower. A family lives in three joined-up train carriages. A man by the lake built a house that is mostly greenhouse, and says he hasn't been cold since.

What you can't do is fence off the lake. Land isn't owned, and paths stay open. The land around homes is looked after the way roads are: by whoever likes doing it, plus a little of everybody's three hours.

## 3. What if I don't like my neighbours?

Move. It costs you a bag and an afternoon.

That changes everything about neighbours. In the old world, a bad neighbour could ruin ten years, because leaving meant selling a house, losing money and maybe changing jobs. Here, most quarrels end with one person deciding they'd rather have a forest view anyway.

Places differ a lot. Some streets are loud, with music late and doors open. Some are quiet by agreement. A few places are protected silences, looked after by the Quiet Army. The map shows which is which.

When two ways of living clash, people talk first. If a drummer lives above someone who sleeps during the day, the usual answer is a soundproofed room at the workshop, not a fight. If talking fails, someone moves. Nobody has to. Usually someone wants to.

As for privacy: your page stays on your own device. Sensors count people. They don't name them. Inside your home there are no sensors unless you put them there.

But I should be honest. A small town is a small town. People notice who visits whom. Pekka once said that enough counting turns into watching, and he was right. [HF036] Neighbours can still be nosy. You can't design that out.

## 4. Where does all the stuff come from?

From people who like making it, and from machines.

There are furniture makers who love wood, tailors who love cloth, bike builders who can't walk past a broken frame. Machines do the heavy and repetitive parts. Every design is public, so any workshop can make anything that has been made before.

Mostly, though, the stuff already exists. Things are built to last for decades. Nobody gets a new kettle every three years. You take one from the store, and when you move, it stays behind or goes back. Much less needs making than you'd think.

Can you ask for something unusual? Yes. Post it. Someone who likes a challenge often picks it up. Even more often, they offer to make it with you, which is usually more fun. Last spring a very tall man asked for a rocking chair that would fit him. Three makers argued about it for a month. The chair is very good.

What if there isn't enough material? Then needs come first. Medicine comes before gadgets. Someone tries to change the design so it uses something else. If that fails, there's a waiting list, and you can see your place on it.

Who decides what gets made? Nobody, centrally. Makers look at what the store is short of and what people are asking for, and make what they like from that. For big factories, a panel decides where they go. [HF035]

## 5. What happens when something breaks?

You have three choices: fix it yourself, ask someone, or swap it.

Everything is built to be opened. Every object carries a note saying what it's made of and how to take it apart. Most people can do small repairs, and there are always patient people to show you.

Every town has repair workshops. The old world had the beginnings of this. In 2009 a woman in Amsterdam started the first Repair Café, where volunteers fixed people's things for free. A few years later there were more than a thousand of them. Here, they never closed.

For something complicated, like a heart monitor or the town's heat pump, there's a list of people who know that machine, along with a record of how their repairs went. You ask the first one.

If it's going to take a while, you swap. You take another one from the store and leave the broken one at the workshop.

Do you owe them? No. There's no ledger. A thank-you is normal. Bringing biscuits is also normal. The people who fix things mostly do it because they enjoy it. Your broken washing machine is their good afternoon.

It took me about a year to stop feeling guilty about that.

## 6. What if I want to do something nobody has done before?

Then you're in the right place. This world was designed for that, even when the thing is useless.

Post the idea. People with the same itch tend to find you, sometimes within the hour. The store has the materials, and the workshops have the tools. The record shows who knows what, so you can find the one person in three towns who understands old glider wings.

Eevi proposed a spaceship centre. The map turned it down in a minute. She built a paper one on the library shed roof instead. [HF035]

Five people near the coast have spent nine years on a wooden aircraft that flaps its wings. It hasn't flown. They don't seem to mind.

There are two limits. The first is the three rules: don't harm, don't deceive, don't get in other people's way. You can risk your own neck, but not your neighbours'. The second is rare materials. If your idea needs a lot of something scarce, someone will ask whether a different design would do. Usually it would.

Nobody asks whether it's useful. Nobody asks whether it will pay. That was the biggest change for me, and the one I took longest to believe.

## 7. What happens when I get old?

You're looked after, by people who like looking after people. Nursing and caring are among the most popular things people do here. Machines do the lifting.

You can stay in your own home, and carers come to you. If you need a lot of help, you can move into a house with others who need the same, where people who like that work live too. Some move in with friends or family. Nobody decides for you.

Medicine is free and open. Much more of it goes into keeping people well, because nobody earns anything from them being ill. [HF006]

Loneliness is still the hardest part. The Loneliness Army visits anyone who'd like company, and many old people are very busy. But some outlive everyone they love, and no design fixes that.

Death still comes. People here talk about it more than they did in the old world. Many plan their last weeks: where, with whom, what music. The old world had hospices too. A nurse and doctor called Cicely Saunders opened one of the first modern ones, in London, in 1967. Here, nobody has to die in a hurry, or in debt.

Aino is in her eighties and learning the cello. She says the worst thing about getting old here is that there's still too much to do.

## 8. Can I just be left alone?

Yes, completely, and nobody expects an explanation.

Nothing is required of anyone. You can spend a year reading, gardening or staring at a lake. You still get food, a home and care. Nobody keeps score.

Hilkka switched off the Earth-monitor eleven years ago. She has no page, no sensors and no name on any list. She lives in the forest north of town, and if you want to talk to her, you write a letter. [HF015]

It has costs, which she chose. She doesn't hear about things, and help is harder to find. So she made her own arrangement: in winter, if her chimney doesn't smoke for two days, her neighbour comes and knocks.

Most people do a smaller version. A quiet month. Monitor-free Sundays. Saying no to every invitation for a season. Nobody comes looking. If someone did, they'd be getting in your way, and that's one of the three rules.

## 9. What about love, relationships and families?

This changed more than I expected.

In the old world, many people stayed together partly because of money. One of them couldn't afford to leave, or neither could afford two homes. Here, nobody needs anyone else's money or house. So couples are together because they want to be, and for no other reason.

That makes some things easier and some harder. It's easier to leave something bad. It's also easier to leave something good on a bad week. People who want to stay sometimes make promises on purpose. Some marry. Some make a vow for one year at a time and renew it. Nobody requires either. [HF039]

Many couples live apart: two flats, next door or across town. The old world already had a name for this, "living apart together", and plenty of people did it then.

Children are raised by their parents and by a lot of other adults. Since nobody has to be at work, there are always people around who like children. Eevi has a learning group, Aino, a library, a river and about thirty people she can ask questions. Nobody gives her marks.

When a relationship ends, nobody loses their home or their income. It still hurts just as much. Maybe more, because you can't blame the money.

Jealousy, heartbreak, and wanting someone who doesn't want you are all still here. Love is the one thing you can't take from the store. [HF014]

## 10. Who looks after the boring things?

The same mix as everything else: people who like it, machines, and a little from everybody.

Some people love drains. Raili is one. Others love power lines, water pumps or roads. Machines do most of the dirty, heavy and dangerous work. Kevin, our small drain robot, does a lot of it. [HF010]

The rest is shared out. If everyone takes a turn at the jobs nobody wants, it comes to about three hours a month each. That's an estimate, and it moves. Last February it said eleven. [HF011]

What if nobody volunteers? Every job has a long list of people who have said "I might do this." If the first person can't, the next one is asked. If the list is empty, the job moves higher on everyone's page until someone takes it. Nasty jobs are cut into small pieces, so nobody does one for long.

How do you know it's being done? The Earth-monitor shows the state of every pipe, cable and road, as it is right now. Anyone can look, and anyone can report something the sensors missed.

It doesn't always work. Three winters ago the heating on the north side was off for eleven days. Sixty people were signed up to fix it, and each one thought someone else had gone. [HF036] Now, when a job is urgent, the Earth-monitor asks one named person first, not sixty. That fixed most of it.

## 11. Can I still have adventures?

Yes. For most people, more than before, because now they have the time.

You can go anywhere. Homes are free to borrow along the way. Boats, tents, climbing gear and bicycles come from the store. People who sail, fly gliders, climb or dive usually like teaching it.

Most people travel slowly. In the old world, many people had two weeks off a year, so they flew. Here, nobody is in a hurry. You can take a whole season to walk to the sea.

There are limits. Long flights use a lot of energy, and the map shows exactly how much, so there are fewer of them and people book them the way they book popular homes. Some wild places are closed in certain seasons, for the birds or for the quiet. A few are closed for good.

Your own safety is your business, as long as you don't put others at risk. If you get into trouble, help comes. The people who rescue climbers here do it because they love mountains.

Is adventure different when you don't need to escape? Yes. In the old world, a lot of travel was a way of getting away from work. Here, people travel towards something instead. Leena has lived in sixty-four homes. She says she has never once been on holiday.

## 12. What happens when people disagree?

Every decision gets the same two questions: how many people does it touch, and how hard is it to undo? [HF012]

Small things are decided by whoever is doing them. Others can object, but only for a real reason: it harms someone, it deceives someone, or it gets in someone's way. Bigger things go to a small group picked by lottery, like a jury. They study the question, explain their decision in public, and then the group ends. Very big things go to panels drawn from many towns, and they go slowly.

Can you challenge a decision? Yes, at any time. A decision only holds until someone has a better idea. So bring the better idea.

Can a community try something completely different? Yes, and it's encouraged. New ideas are tried in one place first. If they work, others copy them. If they don't, nobody else has to.

When two groups can't agree, they often both get to try. Two schools. Two versions of the market. One street with streetlights, and the next one without.

Some things can't be split. There is only one river and only one sky. Those get the slowest and most careful process there is, and the people living nearest can't simply be outvoted.

And if you really can't stand how your town does things, you can start your own version next door. Nobody can stop you, and nobody needs to fight over who's in control, because nobody is.

## And the ones I didn't think to ask

My notebook stopped at twelve. These are the ones I wish I'd added.

**What do I do with everything I own?** Keep what you love. Everything else goes to the store, where someone else will love it. I kept a radio and a coat. I gave away four hundred books, and then borrowed thirty of them back.

**Can I still win at things?** Yes. There are races, chess clubs, and a fierce yearly pie contest. Winning gets you nothing except having won. Most people find that was what they wanted all along.

**What if I'm not good at anything?** Most people aren't, at first. Nobody here needs you to be good at something before you get dinner. You can be a beginner at ten things for the rest of your life.

**Who teaches the children?** Everyone who wants to. Children learn in small groups, following what they're curious about, with adults who like teaching. Eevi's group recently spent a week on whether anyone was happy in the old world. [HF029]

**What if I don't like it here?** Some people don't. They can live quietly, move to a town that does things differently, or start their own. A few seek out places that keep the old ways going for fun. There's a market town on the coast that uses money on Saturdays, as a game. It's popular with people over eighty.

## The last question

At the bottom of the page, in smaller writing, I'd added a thirteenth.

*Will I miss anything?*

Yes. Not what I expected.

I didn't miss money, or shops, or my job. I missed being paid. Not the money itself. The feeling of someone saying, in numbers, that what I'd done had counted.

It took me about a year to stop needing that. Aino says some people never stop, and that's what thank-yous are for.

Now when I fix a drain with Raili, nobody pays me and nobody counts. But on the way home, someone usually says thanks.

Once, it was Pekka.
