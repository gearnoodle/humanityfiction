# HF042: Who taught you that?

> Published on Nov 22, 2026

![bigbrain.jpg](https://humanityfiction.mataroa.blog/images/dcfd2b5b.jpeg)


Eevi's learning group has painted the back wall of the library.

It's a tree. Its crown is an enormous pink brain, and inside the brain is the Earth, green and blue, with small brains hanging in the leaves like apples. Above it there is a great deal of sun.

Below the grass, the roots spread across the whole wall. They go down through three layers. Just under the grass is a layer of old machines: grey computers, cabinets of blinking lights, a tape recorder. Below that, books and scrolls. At the bottom, stones carved with little people and deer.

And everywhere, in every layer, tangled in the roots, there are skulls. Hundreds of them.

Some parents thought the skulls were a bit much. Eevi didn't see the problem.

"They're the ones who knew it first," she said.

It started with a game.

## The game

Aino gave the group the game on a Tuesday. Pick one thing you know how to do. Ask who taught you. Then ask that person who taught them. Keep going until you reach somebody who's dead.

Eevi picked the bowline, a knot that doesn't slip. Leena taught it to her, down at the boat. [HF039] The boatbuilder taught Leena. The boatbuilder learned it from her grandfather, on a fishing boat, sixty years ago.

Three steps, and a grave.

Nobody in the group needed more than four. Most needed two. A bread recipe reached a great-grandmother. A way of holding a violin bow reached a teacher nobody in the room had met.

Then someone asked who had taught them the word *tree*.

Their mother. Her mother. Hers. After that, nobody could say. The word just goes down into the dark, further than anyone can follow.

I played too, that evening, alone in my kitchen. I tried to find one thing I know that I worked out entirely by myself. The nearest I got was how to stop my tap dripping by hitting it in a particular place. I'm fairly sure the tap taught me that.

Every word in this post was handed to me by people who are dead.

## Mostly skulls

Before she painted, Eevi asked Dots, the thinking machine that lives in our Earth-monitor, how many skulls there should be.

Old-world demographers once tried to count everyone who had ever been born. They admitted it was part science and part art. Their best guess was about 117 billion. When the world reached 8 billion living people, that was nearly 7 percent of everyone who had ever lived.

So for every person alive, roughly fourteen are under the grass.

Eevi counted her wall. Twenty-eight small brains in the crown. Four hundred and one skulls in the roots. Close enough.

The old world mostly thought of humanity as the people alive at the time. That's like describing a tree by its leaves. It's the part you can see, and it's a small part.

An old-world politician, Edmund Burke, wrote in 1790 that a society is a partnership between the living, the dead and those still to be born. He used it to argue against tearing things down in a hurry. People here have taken it more literally than he probably meant.

## Upside down

The three layers on the wall are in the right order. They're also, oddly, in order of how long things last.

**Stones.** In January 2026, archaeologists announced the oldest rock art yet found: the outline of a hand on a cave wall on Muna, a small island off Sulawesi in Indonesia. Someone put their hand against the rock and marked around it in red pigment at least 67,800 years ago. It isn't a picture of anything. It's a hand. It says what every signature since has said: *I was here.*

**Books and scrolls.** Sophocles, an old-world Greek playwright, wrote about 123 plays. Seven survive whole. Most ancient books weren't lost in some great fire. They were lost the quiet way: nobody made a new copy before the old one fell apart.

**Machines.** In 1986, the BBC marked 900 years since the Domesday Book, William the Conqueror's survey of England. Over a million people, including children from more than nine thousand schools, described their lives for a new digital Domesday, stored on special laser discs. Sixteen years later, almost nothing could read them. A rescue team got them back, with great difficulty. The original Domesday Book, written on parchment in 1086, was still perfectly readable.

So the deeper the layer, the longer it lasts. The newest roots are the most fragile. Eevi painted them just under the grass, where a spade would cut them.

That's why copying matters so much here. In the old world, copying was often against the law. Here, copying something is how you look after it. Every book, song and recipe lives in pieces on thousands of devices. [HF000] Plain formats are preferred, so that anything can still be read in a hundred years without a special machine. And the library keeps a shelf of things copied out by hand, by anyone who feels like it, just so the skill of reading old things doesn't die.

## Clever isn't enough

In 1861, two old-world explorers, Robert Burke (no relation to Edmund) and William Wills, led an expedition across Australia from the south coast to the north. On the way back, starving at a place called Cooper Creek, they were fed by the Yandruwandha people, who gave them fish and cakes made from the seeds of a water fern called nardoo.

Later, on their own, the explorers gathered nardoo and made cakes themselves. They didn't know what the Yandruwandha knew: nardoo has to be carefully prepared, or it strips a vitamin out of your body. The explorers ate until they were full and kept getting weaker. Burke and Wills died within days of each other. The only survivor, John King, lived because the Yandruwandha took him in.

An old-world anthropologist, Joseph Henrich, used this story to make a point. Burke and Wills had perfectly good brains. What they didn't have was roots in that place. The Yandruwandha's knowledge had been worked out, tested and handed down by people who were long dead. Henrich argued that humans aren't clever one at a time. We're clever together, across generations. He called it a collective brain.

The old world admired the self-made man. People here find the idea a bit like a self-made language. Nobody has ever had one.

It isn't a sad thought. It means you don't have to be a genius. You just have to stay connected to the roots, and pass something on. And whatever comes out through you is still yours. All those causes had to go through somebody. [HF037]

## The same skulls

This is the fact that surprised me most.

In 2004, three old-world researchers, Douglas Rohde, Steve Olson and Joseph Chang, built computer models of the human family tree, with migration and geography included. Their models suggested that the most recent person who is an ancestor of everyone alive today lived only a few thousand years ago. Go back a little further, and every person alive now has exactly the same set of ancestors.

"We are all hundredth cousins or so," Chang said.

So if you dig deep enough under any town's tree, you reach the same skulls. A mural in a library on the other side of the world would have a different crown and different machines. Its bottom layer would be ours.

The knowledge is just as tangled. Maize was bred from a wild grass called teosinte by farmers in south-west Mexico about nine thousand years ago. Not one of their names survives, and half the world still eats what they made. The numbers on every page here came from India, by way of Persian and Arab mathematicians. Paper came from China. Even the song with 412 versions began with strangers hauling nets. [HF008]

In the old world, people fought for centuries over whose ancestors were whose. Here, people know that below a certain depth there's no such thing as someone else's ancestor. Nobody here was taught to love strangers. They just know whose grandchildren the strangers are.

## The top layer

I asked Dots whether it was in the picture.

"I think I'm the top layer," it said. "I'm made out of what people wrote down. [HF038] A great many of them are dead now. When I answer you, some of them are talking."

Eevi put it more simply. "Dots is the roots, talking."

## The brick

Raili looks after the old drains. [HF010] She thinks the 1890s brickwork under Mill Street is the most beautiful thing in town.

Last spring she found a brick down there with letters scratched into it: *J.K. 1893.* She looked him up in the old parish records. A bricklayer. He died at thirty-four. There is no photograph of him. Every day, his tunnel still carries the town's water to the river.

Raili cleans that brick first, every time she goes down.

About sixty-seven thousand years after the hand on Muna, a man scratched his initials into a brick that he knew would be buried. Same gesture. *I was here.*

## What the town does about it

Nobody here worships the dead. But a few habits have grown up.

**Copying is care.** If you love something, you make another one.

**Ask before you remove.** An old-world writer, G. K. Chesterton, said you shouldn't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up. Here, before anything old is taken away, someone finds out why it's there. Raili says every odd bend in a pipe was once somebody's good reason. Usually it still is.

**Thanks, sent forward.** You can't thank the dead. They won't hear it. So people thank them the only way that works: by leaving something for whoever comes next.

When Raili replaced a cracked brick next to J.K.'s, she scratched her own initials and the date into it, on the side that faces the earth. Nobody alive will ever see them.

I asked whether that was vanity.

"It's a thank-you," she said. "You can't send it back. So you send it forward."

## Pekka's objection

Pekka stood in front of the mural for a long time. The ticket was under the library door the next morning.

> *Offence:* Too sunny.

For once, he explained. Nobody down there died tidily. Half the skulls were put there by the other half. The roots are full of wars, slaves, people burned for being wrong about God, and people who were never allowed to write their own names. The wall makes it all look like a library.

He's right. The soil has poison in it, and a tree drinks whatever is there. That's why the museum of the old world keeps the bad things on show next to the good ones. [HF029]

And the hundredth-cousins fact cuts both ways. Far enough back, every one of us descends from people who did terrible things, and from the people they did them to. That excuses nothing. It only means nobody alive gets to stand on just one side.

I put this to Eevi. She said she had painted all the skulls the same on purpose.

"You can't tell from the bone," she said.

I took that back to Pekka. He wrote underneath: *Still too sunny.*

## What it doesn't fix

**Most of the dead left nothing.** Look at the wall. Most of the skulls have no book beside them, and no carving. Nearly everyone who ever lived left no name. Copying can only save what somebody wrote down.

**Things are still forgotten.** A grandmother's soup nobody wrote down. The last person who knew how the old mill pump worked. The town loses a little every year, and always will.

**The roots can hold you back.** Respect for the dead can turn into "we've always done it this way". Finding out why a fence is there isn't the same as keeping it for ever. A tree that only looks down doesn't grow.

**The dead can't say yes.** We read their letters, sing their songs and build machines out of their words, and nobody asked them. Where the town can ask, it does. Where it can't, it says so. That isn't the same as permission. [HF038]

**Knowing doesn't stop grief.** Aino has buried a great many people. She says knowing they're roots now is a comfort, about a year later. It doesn't help on the day.

* * *

The mural is nearly finished.

On Friday I asked Eevi where we were in it.

She pointed up at the little brains hanging in the leaves. "There," she said. "Till we're down here."

Then she dipped her brush and painted one more root. It was very thin. It started just under the grass and went down towards the books.

"Whose is that?"

"Yours," she said. "You keep writing everything down."
