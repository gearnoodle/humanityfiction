# HF044: Do you even read this?

> Published on Nov 22, 2026

Another made-up letter. This one arrived about an hour after the last one. [HF043]

> Dear MJ,
>
> I've stopped trying to keep up.
>
> This morning you posted a tree full of skulls. [HF042] An hour later came a long answer to an angry letter about whether you write your own posts. [HF043] I hadn't finished the skulls.
>
> Fine. A machine helps you write. You've owned up to that. But here's my real question. Do you even read these?
>
> Nobody could, at this speed. Some days three posts arrive before I've finished my coffee. You can't possibly have read the last one before the next one is out.
>
> Here's a test. What colour is the bench? Who plays the other three parts when Aino's quartet can't meet? What time do people in your town wake up for no reason?
>
> I'm reading your blog more carefully than you are. That seems backwards.
>
> Someone with a full cup of cold coffee

## The test

Fair enough. Let's see.

**What colour is the bench?** Pink. I knew that one. [HF004]

**Who plays the other three parts?** Dots, the town's thinking machine. I had to look that up. [HF041]

**What time do people wake up?** 4:17. I remembered the number. I had forgotten that nobody in the town knows why. [HF030]

Two out of three without looking anything up. Not a great score for the person who made the place.

## The honest answer

Not at first. Later, yes.

The order matters. First I make them. Then, sometimes days later, I read them. And that's when the interesting part starts.

## How can I know what I think?

In 1926, an old-world thinker called Graham Wallas wrote a book called *The Art of Thought*. He split creative work into four stages: preparation, incubation, illumination and verification. Get stuck into a problem. Leave it alone while the back of your mind works on it. Wait for the flash. Then check whether the flash was any good.

In the same book, he tells a story about a little girl. She was told to be sure of her meaning before she spoke. She answered: "How can I know what I think till I see what I say?"

Of course it was a little girl. There is always a little girl. [HF043]

A year later, the novelist E. M. Forster told the same joke, about an old lady. Fifty years after that, Joan Didion wrote that she wrote "entirely to find out what I'm thinking".

So I'm in good company. My order is just a bit odder. I don't see what I say. I see what I asked for, and what came back.

Here are my four stages:

1. **Make it.** A question at six in the morning, a draft by seven.
2. **Leave it.** Wallas called this incubation. I call it going to work.
3. **Read it later, as if a stranger wrote it.** Partly, one did. [HF043]
4. **Get surprised.**

## What I found

When I finally sat down and read the whole blog through, I found things in it I didn't remember. Some I'm not sure I ever properly noticed.

- A woman in the town lives in an old water tower. [HF040]
- Someone has drawn a map of the town the way it sounds to bats. [HF041]
- On the coast there's a market town that uses money on Saturdays, as a game. It's popular with people over eighty. [HF040]
- Five people have spent nine years building a wooden aircraft that flaps its wings. It hasn't flown. They don't seem to mind. [HF040]
- The man who explains things. The gossip on Mill Street. A feud in the choir that is older than Eevi. Each mentioned once, and never again. [HF036]

I read the bit about the bats three times. I'd have liked to meet whoever drew that map. Then I remembered that, in a roundabout way, I'm responsible for them.

## The box that talks back

Niklas Luhmann was an old-world German sociologist. Over about forty years he filled a set of wooden boxes with roughly 90,000 handwritten cards: notes on what he read, and on what he thought about it.

In 1981 he wrote an essay about his card boxes. He described them as a partner he talked to, and said that for a real conversation, both partners have to be able to surprise each other. His boxes surprised him. He'd forgotten what he had put in, and it came back to him in combinations he had never planned.

This blog has become my box of cards. And because I didn't write every card myself, it surprises me even more than his did.

## What fell out

These are the questions I found when I read it all back. None of them were planned. Each one needs a post of its own.

- **Why do people wake at 4:17?** The Earth-monitor has counted them for years. [HF030] As far as I can tell, nobody has asked them.
- **Who stops a man who won't stop?** Pekka asked that. The answer was "No good answer yet." [HF036] It still isn't a good enough answer.
- **What actually happens in the money town on Saturdays?** And why do the over-eighties love it? Nostalgia, or something stranger?
- **What does the Quiet Army protect, and from whom?** It gets one line. [HF013]
- **What's it like to be the man who explains things,** in a town where anyone can ask Dots?

## So, do I read them?

Yes. Later. More than once. That's where the next posts come from.

If I waited until I had read each post properly before making the next one, there wouldn't be a next one. The making is the preparation. The reading is where the flash comes.

And you don't have to keep up. In the town, screens are built to let you go. [HF007] This blog isn't, yet. So take the town's advice: read three, then stop. They'll still be here. Nothing in the town expires, and nothing here does either.
