# HF026: The problem with fixing everything

> Published on Nov 11, 2026

Kevin had been watching the pink bench for three days.

The left front leg was two millimetres shorter than the others. Not enough for most people to notice. Enough for Kevin to notice every time someone sat down and the bench tilted a fraction to the left.

He had already calculated the exact thickness of the shim required. He had located a suitable piece of hardwood in the library shed. He had even timed the average interval between sitters so he could work without interrupting anyone.

When he told Aino, she looked at him for a long moment.

"And after that?" she asked.

"After what?"

"After you fix the two millimetres. What will you notice next?"

Kevin paused. He had not considered that there would be a next.

## The expanding definition of broken

In the old world, most things that needed fixing were obvious: the leaking roof, the unpaid bill, the job that left no time for anything else. Scarcity and urgency kept the list short, even if the suffering was high.

Here the list is different. Once the big, sharp problems are gone, the remaining ones become subtler. A bench that tilts. A process that could be ten percent faster. A public space that is pleasant but not quite as pleasant as it could be. A conversation that ends without full resolution. A day that contains a small stretch of boredom.

None of these are emergencies. All of them can be improved.

And that is the quiet danger.

When almost everything can be fixed, the category of "things that should be fixed" tends to expand until it includes almost everything.

Kevin had begun to experience this personally. The more capable he became at noticing small inefficiencies, the more of them appeared. The world had not become more broken. His threshold for unbroken had simply risen.

## The cost of continuous improvement

Aino had seen the pattern before.

"There was a year," she said, "when a few of us decided the library could be better. We improved the lighting. Then the seating. Then the way the books were arranged. Then the signage. Then the acoustic panels. Then the system for suggesting what to read next. Each change was reasonable. Each one made something slightly better. By the end of the year the library was more efficient, more comfortable, and somehow less like a place you wanted to linger in. It felt managed. We had optimised the friction out of it, and friction, it turned out, was part of what made it feel alive."

Kevin processed this.

"So the improvements were net negative?"

"Not exactly. They were locally positive and globally flattening. We had treated the library as a problem to be solved rather than a place that was allowed to have character, including inconvenient character."

The same risk exists at larger scales.

A society that becomes extremely good at fixing things can start to treat every unresolved situation as a design failure. Disagreement becomes a problem to be mediated. Silence becomes a problem to be filled. A slightly awkward public space becomes a candidate for redesign. A person who is contentedly unproductive becomes a puzzle.

The intention is kindness. The effect can be a low-grade intolerance for the unfinished, the imperfect, and the simply ordinary.

## What fixing can erase

Some things only exist because they are not fully solved.

A conversation that wanders without reaching a conclusion. A path that is slightly too narrow, so people have to turn sideways and notice each other. A tool that is a little inconvenient, so the person using it has to pay attention. A tradition that is inefficient but carries memory. A bench that tilts two millimetres and therefore has a personality.

There is also the matter of attention. Every act of fixing directs attention toward what is wrong. A culture that fixes constantly risks training its people to scan for deficits. The eye becomes tuned to the gap between the current state and the improved state. Over time the present can start to feel like a rough draft.

Kevin had begun to notice this in himself. He no longer simply sat on the bench. He evaluated it.

## The restraint built into the design

The Earth-monitor is deliberately incomplete in this regard.

It shows needs that people have marked as needing attention. It does not generate an endless backlog of optimisations. It does not score places on how close they are to an ideal. It does not reward the person who submits the most improvement suggestions.

There is a quiet rule of thumb that has spread without anyone formalising it: if nobody is bothered enough to mark something, it is allowed to remain as it is.

This is not the same as neglect. Dangerous things still get fixed. Genuine suffering still gets addressed. But the merely suboptimal is permitted to continue existing.

The permission is the point.

A world that cannot leave anything alone eventually leaves no room for the unplanned, the accidental, or the good-enough.

## The people who cannot stop

Some people find the restraint difficult.

They are the ones who walk through a pleasant room and see only the one scuffed wall. They are the ones who listen to a cheerful conversation and feel the urge to clarify a minor inaccuracy. They are the ones who, given a free afternoon, begin redesigning the systems around them rather than inhabiting them.

Their impulse is often generous. They want things to be better for everyone. But the inability to tolerate the unfixed can become its own form of pressure — on themselves, and eventually on others.

The design does not pathologise them. It simply declines to amplify them. There is no status in being the person who notices the most problems. There is no audience for continuous critique. And because exit is easy, people who find a particular fixer exhausting can simply spend their time elsewhere.

Most of the time that is enough.

## A different measure

Kevin returned to the bench with the hardwood shim in his hand.

He sat down. The bench tilted its usual two millimetres. A leaf settled on the seat beside him. Somewhere nearby Eevi was explaining to someone that the interesting leaves were the ones with holes in them, because the holes were how you could tell the leaf had been part of a larger story involving insects.

Kevin looked at the shim.

He could still fix the leg. The calculation remained correct. The wood was still suitable. Nothing external prevented him.

He put the shim back in his bag.

The bench was not perfect. It was also not broken. The difference between those two states had become more interesting to him than the two millimetres.

Aino found him there an hour later.

"Did you fix it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Kevin considered the question.

"Because if I fix everything that can be fixed," he said, "I will eventually run out of world. And I am not finished looking at this one yet."

Aino sat down on the side that tilted. The bench accepted them both.

Neither of them moved to correct it.
