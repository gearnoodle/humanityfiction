# HF011: Three hours a month

> Published on Nov 1, 2026

Eevi found an old careers guide at the library. Hundreds of job titles, from the old world. She brought it over to the pink bench and started guessing.

- **Brand manager.** "Someone who looks after cows?"
- **Telemarketer.** "A market... on the telephone? For fish?"
- **Parking enforcement officer.** "Do cars not want to park?"
- **Landlord.** "Lord of the land? Like a king?" Basically, yes.
- **Middle manager.** "Who manages the edges, then?"
- **Influencer.** "Influences what?" Mostly what people buy. "Why?"
- **Insurance claims adjuster.** She gave up on this one.
- **Loss prevention specialist.** "Someone who stops you losing things! Can I be one?" Then I explained it meant guarding shops. She looked betrayed.

## So what work is left?

There's a small number in the corner of the Earth-monitor:

> *If everyone did about 3 hours a month, everything that truly needs doing would get done.*

Nobody is asked to. It's just there, like the temperature.

Three hours sounds like nothing. But that's only the **needed work**: the drains, the cleaning, the night shifts, the jobs nobody especially wants. Machines do most of it, and the rest is shared thinly across everyone.

Then there's **chosen work**: caring for children and old people, art, research, fixing, growing food, teaching. Most people do lots of it and never call it work. Aino stitches people. Raili climbs into sewers. Nobody counts those hours.

And then there's **play**, which, it turns out, is where a lot of the chosen work comes from anyway.

## Where all the time went

In 1930, the economist Keynes guessed that by about now, people might work three-hour shifts. Decades later, the anthropologist David Graeber wrote a whole book arguing that many jobs felt pointless even to the people doing them.

Both were onto something. A huge share of old-world work existed only because of how the old world was built:

- **Because of money:** banking, trading, billing, debt collecting, cashiers, ticket inspectors.
- **Because someone owned the scarce thing:** landlords, patent lawyers, shop security.
- **Because people had to be persuaded to want things:** mass advertising, telemarketing, sales targets.
- **Because things broke:** disposable goods, packaging, fast fashion.
- **Because there was someone to manage:** reports nobody read, reorganisations, job interviews.

Take away the reasons, and the jobs quietly dissolve.

## What survived in a new shape

Some old jobs did a real thing underneath. That part stayed.

- **Advertising became discovery.** "Here's something new, in case you'd like it." Like the song family tree, or a friend saying "you'd love this."
- **Branding became a maker's mark.** A reputation for good work, not a logo you paid for.
- **Commercial fashion became natural fashion.** Making, mending, swapping, and wild personal style with no trend calendar telling anyone what to wear.
- **Service became hospitality.** Nobody serves the rich anymore. But people still open cafés, cook for strangers and host long dinners, because it's a joy.

* * *

In the end, Eevi decided to become a loss prevention specialist anyway.

She now helps people find lost mittens.
