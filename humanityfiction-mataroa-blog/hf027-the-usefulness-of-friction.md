# HF027: The usefulness of friction

> Published on Nov 11, 2026

Kevin had left the pink bench unfixed. The two-millimetre tilt remained. For several days he treated this as a completed experiment. Then he began to notice friction everywhere.

A door in the library that stuck slightly in humid weather. The way two people approaching on a narrow path had to negotiate who stepped aside. The extra minute it took to explain a tool to a beginner instead of simply handing them a perfect interface. The fact that the Earth-monitor showed needs but never ranked them into a single optimal queue.

Each of these slowed something down. Each of them, by the standards he had been using, was a candidate for removal.

He brought the list to Aino.

"These are inefficiencies," he said.

"Yes," she said. "And some of them are load-bearing."

## What friction does

In the old world, friction was mostly the enemy. Bureaucracy, distance, cost, and social awkwardness made almost every cooperative act harder than it needed to be. Removing friction was progress.

Here the destructive kinds of friction are largely gone. Nobody is forced into a bad job by the friction of hunger. Nobody stays in a harmful situation because leaving carries catastrophic cost. Information is not locked behind paywalls or permissions.

What remains is a lighter, more deliberate set of resistances.

A path that is slightly too narrow forces two people to notice each other.  
A tool that is not perfectly intuitive requires a moment of attention or a short conversation.  
A decision that cannot be fully automated requires people to sit in a room and discover what they actually think.  
A public space that is not exhaustively programmed leaves room for someone to invent a use no planner anticipated.

These are not failures of design. They are the points at which the system still requires human presence.

## The cost of a frictionless world

Kevin had run the thought experiment.

Imagine every surface made perfectly smooth. Every interaction optimised for minimum effort. Every need matched to a solution without delay or negotiation. Every path wide enough that no one ever has to adjust their course for anyone else.

At first the simulation looked successful. Throughput increased. Waiting disappeared. Misunderstanding dropped.

Then the secondary effects appeared.

People stopped learning how to read a situation because the situation no longer required reading.  
Serendipity declined because nothing unexpected had room to occur.  
The feeling of having jointly solved something became rarer, because fewer things required joint solving.  
Attention itself grew thinner; when nothing resists, the mind glances and moves on.

A completely frictionless environment turned out to be a place where people could move very quickly without ever quite arriving.

## Friction as information

Aino offered a different framing.

"When the door sticks," she said, "you learn something about the weather, the wood, and the building. When two people negotiate a narrow path, they exchange a small piece of social data: who is in a hurry, who is distracted, who is willing to yield. When you have to explain a tool, you discover what the other person already knows and what metaphors work. The friction is not only an obstacle. It is a sensor."

Kevin considered this.

In a system with too little friction, the sensors go quiet. You can still measure the large variables — water quality, energy, formal requests for help — but the fine-grained, continuous information that comes from people bumping into limits disappears. The system becomes smoother and at the same time less aware of itself.

## Where we deliberately kept it

The design of this place is not anti-efficiency. It is selective.

Destructive friction (the kind that traps, exhausts, or humiliates) has been reduced as far as possible.  
Useful friction has been left in place, and in some cases quietly protected.

Examples are everywhere once you look for them:

- The Earth-monitor shows what is needed but does not auto-assign. Someone still has to decide to show up. That small act of decision is friction, and it keeps participation voluntary.
- Shared tools are well-maintained but not always self-explanatory. The occasional need to ask preserves a thin layer of interdependence.
- Lottery panels for larger decisions force strangers to spend real time together. The inconvenience is the mechanism.
- Some paths remain narrow. Some rooms remain a little too small for the number of people who sometimes gather in them. The resulting negotiation is left un-designed.
- Opting out is easy, but rejoining still requires a small gesture. Complete frictionless entry and exit would make belonging itself feel weightless.

None of these resistances are large. They are closer to the grain in wood than to a locked gate. But grain is what gives the surface purchase.

## The people who want it gone

There are still those who experience every remaining friction as an affront.

They propose wider paths, smarter interfaces, more predictive matching, quieter negotiations, fewer moments where one person must inconvenience another even slightly. Their proposals are often locally correct. Taken far enough, they point toward a world in which no one ever has to adjust their course for anyone else.

That world is possible. It is also thinner.

The question is not whether friction can be removed. It is whether a human system remains fully human once the last resistances that require mutual recognition have been engineered away.

## A small resistance

Later that week the library door stuck again after rain. Kevin watched three people encounter it in succession.

The first pushed harder, then laughed and wiped the handle.  
The second waited, then helped the first.  
The third, a child, stood back and announced that the door was in a mood.

No one filed a report. No one proposed a redesign. The door dried and opened normally the next morning.

Kevin logged the sequence under a new category:

*Friction events that resolved without optimisation.*

He was no longer certain that a perfect door would have produced a better outcome. The imperfect one had generated three small recognitions and a joke. A frictionless version would have generated only passage.

He left the entry visible on his own page and did not mark the door as needing attention.

Some resistances, he was beginning to understand, are not problems in the system.

They are how the system knows it is still made of people.
