# HF030: The second shrug

> Published on Nov 11, 2026

On Monday a woman set up a table by the library, on the exact spot where the courgette man once tried to sell courgettes.

She had a sign: *Become your best self. Free.*

It didn't go well. Everything here is free, so that part impressed nobody. And people kept asking what was wrong with the self they'd come with.

Her name is Ritva. In the old world she was a life coach. (Eevi, on hearing this: "A coach is a bus. Did she drive people's lives?" More or less.)

It got me thinking about the second shrug.

## Two shrugs

The Great Shrug was really two. People shrugged at the things they'd been told to want: the phone, the house, the followers. And they shrugged at everyone telling them to be better.

The museum only has a photo of the first one. The second left nothing to photograph. It happened inside people, mostly lying down.

## The list

The old world had a list for you. It went roughly:

Be kinder. Try harder. Buy less. Wake earlier. Drink more water. Be grateful. Be present. Save the planet. Reach your potential. Also, relax.

Nobody ever finished the list. That was the clever part. It came from every direction, it renewed itself every January, and it was usually delivered by someone who hadn't finished it either.

## Why this shrug is harder

**The list is mostly true.** Kindness is good. Water is good. Shrugging at a shopping advert costs nothing. Shrugging at "be kinder" feels like shrugging at kindness.

**It came from people who loved you.** Adverts came from strangers. The list came from parents, teachers and friends, and they meant it.

**It moved in.** After enough years, nobody had to say it. You said it to yourself, in your own voice, usually at night.

**Feeling bad felt like doing something.** Guilt was a kind of payment. You didn't change, but at least you'd suffered a bit about it, which seemed only fair.

## What it's worth

Here is what people slowly noticed.

The list never worked. Not on them, and not on anyone they knew. People were being asked to be saints inside a setup that made it hard, and then blamed when they weren't.

And the guilt wasn't free. A person busy failing their own list has very little attention left for anybody else. The old world's psychologists found something that surprised them: people who went easy on themselves after a failure were *more* likely to try again, not less. The harsh ones mostly gave up, or hid.

So the shrug didn't make people worse. It gave them their attention back. And attention, pointed outward, is most of what kindness is.

There's a second gain. When nobody is told to be kind, the kindness you meet is real. Nobody is performing it for the list.

## What the shrug isn't

It isn't giving up on getting better *at* things. Aino is learning the cello and is, by her own account, terrible. Raili knows more about sewer brickwork every year. Getting better at something is play. Being told to get better *as a person* is a debt, and nobody ever tells you the amount.

And it doesn't cover harm. "You should be more patient" is a sermon. "You hurt me yesterday" is a report. Reports, you listen to. The three rules never shrugged at anything.

I notice I've now spent a page telling you what to do. Shrug at that too.

## A theory about 4:17

For years the Earth-monitor has listed an unexplained number of people waking at 4:17 in the morning. Nobody knows why.

I have a theory. I think they're the ones who still have the list. They wake up, and they go through it.

Aino calls it the last inherited habit: the belief that a life has to justify itself.

* * *

Ritva packed up around noon. Aino brought her tea and sat down.

"Nobody here wants to be better," Ritva said.

"No," said Aino. "They're all right."

"I don't know how to be all right. I only know how to be improving."

Eevi had been listening. She looked Ritva up and down, the way she inspects a leaf.

"What's wrong with your self?" she asked.

Ritva opened her mouth, and then didn't say anything for quite a long time.

On Thursday the Earth-monitor showed the 4:17 number down by one.

I can't prove anything. Sensors count. They never identify.
