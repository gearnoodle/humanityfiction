# HF015: Off the monitor

> Published on Nov 2, 2026

On the Earth-monitor map, just north of town, there's a grey patch in the forest. It has a small label: *someone's quiet.*

That's where Hilkka lives. She switched the Earth-monitor off eleven years ago. No page, no sensors on her land, no name on any list.

I wanted to meet her, but there was no way to send a message. So I wrote a letter on paper, walked out, and left it in the old mailbox by her gate. Three weeks later, a reply came: *Thursday. Bring nothing.*

## Is that allowed?

Yes. Completely.

Taking part is a hobby, and that includes the Earth-monitor itself. Nobody has to have a page, be counted, or be findable. Switching it off takes one minute. Switching it back on takes one minute too, and nothing is lost in between.

In the old world, opting out was almost impossible. Phones, cards, cameras and apps tracked people whether they liked it or not. Here, it's a switch. Maybe that's the real test of any system: how easy is it to leave?

And it doesn't have to be all or nothing. Lots of people take quiet weeks, or monitor-free Sundays. Some keep their page but turn off sensors at home. Hilkka just went all the way.

## Why people switch off

Hilkka gave me her reasons over coffee.

"Too much world," she said. "It showed me every broken thing within fifty kilometres. I wanted to help with all of it, and I couldn't sleep."

Others switch off for different reasons. Some want silence. Some remember the old world, when being watched meant being sold, and they never quite trusted anything with a screen again. Some just want to be unknown for a while. That's allowed to be enough.

## What it costs her

She doesn't see the needs, or the offers. She missed the whole courgette surplus. She didn't know about the pink bench until I told her. ("Pink? Good.")

It's harder for her to find company, or help.

And if she gets ill, no sensor will notice. So she made her own arrangement, the old-fashioned way. In winter, if her chimney doesn't smoke for two days, her nearest neighbour comes and knocks. Hilkka asked for that. It isn't surveillance. It's a deal between two people.

## What it costs everyone else

The river survey has a gap where her forest is. Her sixty years of knowing that land is off the record.

Does she get free food, care and everything else without giving anything back to the system? Yes. And that's fine. Nothing is expected of anyone, hermits included.

But opting out of being watched doesn't mean opting out of the three rules. She can't harm, deceive or block anyone either. And emergencies are still emergencies. The sensors stop at her fence, but smoke doesn't. If her forest catches fire, help comes anyway.

## Why hermits matter

A world like this needs its Hilkkas.

People switching off is a kind of signal. If a few do, the system is healthy: the door is open, and some people walk through it. If many suddenly do, something has gone wrong. Maybe the pages got too pushy, or something started to feel like the old world. The hermits notice first.

They also remind everyone that a life doesn't have to be visible to be real.

* * *

As I was leaving, Hilkka handed me a folded piece of paper.

"Tell whoever's in charge of the river," she said, "the pike are back in the north bay."

"Nobody's in charge."

"Then tell everyone."

So I did. I posted it on the Earth-monitor, signed *someone off the monitor*.
