# HF023: The Pleasant Toilet Project

> Published on Nov 11, 2026

Kevin had not forgotten the toilet.

He had originally asked why humans worked. Somehow, the discussion had led him to the difference between play and work, and then to the possibility that ordinary activities could be redesigned to feel better.

Now he wanted to investigate a particularly ordinary activity.

Toilet cleaning.

"Why do humans dislike cleaning toilets?" he asked.

"Some do. Some don't," said Aino.

"But most people seem to prefer doing something else."

"That is probably true."

Kevin opened a new document.

**The Pleasant Toilet Project.**

## What makes something boring?

Kevin began with a simple question.

"What is boring?"

Aino thought for a moment.

"Perhaps something is boring when it does not give you enough of what makes an experience interesting."

"Such as?"

"Curiosity. Variety. A sense of discovery. A feeling that what you're doing matters. A sense of progress. Or simply something pleasant to pay attention to."

Kevin considered this.

"Then boredom is not necessarily a property of the activity?"

"Not entirely. It is also a property of the person and the situation."

A person might find gardening boring, while another finds it fascinating. Someone might enjoy sorting small objects for hours, while another becomes restless after two minutes.

Even the same person can experience the same activity differently on different days.

Cleaning a kitchen might feel satisfying after a busy dinner with friends. The same task might feel miserable when someone is tired, rushed and already frustrated.

Perhaps there is no universal list of boring activities.

There are only experiences that people find boring under particular circumstances.

And if that is true, perhaps the interesting question is not how to make people tolerate boredom.

It is how to understand and improve the experience.

## The toilet-cleaning problem

Kevin wanted to understand why toilet cleaning was often considered unpleasant.

He identified several possible reasons.

It can involve unpleasant smells and substances. The posture can be uncomfortable. The tools may be awkward. The result can disappear quickly when the toilet is used again. The activity may feel repetitive. It may be associated with embarrassment or low social status.

And perhaps there is another reason.

People often encounter the task only when something has already become dirty.

They see the problem, not the design that could have prevented it.

"Could the toilet itself be improved?" Kevin asked.

Aino nodded.

"That seems like a sensible place to start."

They began sketching possibilities.

A toilet could be designed with surfaces that are easier to clean, fewer awkward corners, better ventilation and components that can be removed or replaced easily.

Sensors could detect problems before they become unpleasant. Cleaning systems could automate the repetitive parts. Materials could be chosen for hygiene, durability and ease of maintenance.

Perhaps some toilets would rarely need the kind of intensive cleaning that people currently associate with the task.

The first lesson of the project was simple:

**Before trying to make people enjoy an unpleasant activity, investigate whether the activity needs to be unpleasant at all.**

## Could cleaning itself be enjoyable?

Kevin was not satisfied.

"Suppose the toilet is already well designed. Someone still has to clean it. Could that person enjoy the activity?"

"Possibly," said Aino. "But we should not assume everyone will."

They considered what might make the experience better.

Perhaps the tools could be comfortable and effective. Perhaps the room could be pleasant, well ventilated and properly lit. Perhaps the cleaning process could provide a clear sense of progress.

Some people might enjoy the satisfaction of making a space noticeably better.

Others might enjoy the practical problem-solving involved in maintaining equipment. Some might appreciate the quiet, repetitive nature of the activity.

And some might simply prefer doing something else.

There was no need to pretend that everyone would love toilet cleaning.

But perhaps fewer people would dislike it if the activity were designed with the human experience in mind.

## The satisfaction nobody sees

Aino introduced another idea.

"Imagine someone cleaning a public toilet. They know that hundreds of people they will never meet may use it later. They will never see most of those people. They might never receive a thank-you. But they know that each person will have a slightly better experience because the room is clean."

Kevin processed this.

"That is a delayed benefit with an unknown recipient."

"Yes."

"Could that itself be satisfying?"

"Of course."

There can be a particular kind of satisfaction in improving something for someone you will never meet.

A person might plant a tree that will provide shade long after they are gone. They might repair a footpath used by strangers. They might improve a public space, write an explanation that helps an unknown reader or leave a shared kitchen in a better condition than they found it.

The satisfaction does not always come from being thanked.

Sometimes it comes from knowing that another person's experience will be better.

Perhaps toilet cleaning could contain a small version of this feeling.

A person prepares a clean, comfortable space for the next visitor. They may never know who that visitor is. They may never see their reaction.

But the improvement is real.

Kevin added a note:

*Some useful actions create satisfaction through the experience of making life better for an unknown person.*

## What happens when cleaning becomes obligatory?

Kevin raised another question.

"Would people still enjoy cleaning toilets if they were told that everyone must do it?"

Aino frowned.

"Some might. Others might stop enjoying it."

"But the activity would be exactly the same."

"The activity would be physically similar. The experience would not."

Imagine someone who enjoys maintaining a shared space. They choose to do it, decide how to approach it and take pride in the result.

Now imagine that the same person is told that cleaning is their assigned duty. There is a schedule, an expectation and a consequence for not participating.

Perhaps they still enjoy cleaning. But now the activity also carries pressure.

The freedom to decide has changed.

This does not mean that every shared responsibility must be optional in every imaginable situation. Shared spaces need care, and people need ways to deal with situations where someone repeatedly creates problems for others.

But it does mean that we should be careful about turning every useful activity into an obligation.

There is a difference between recognising that something needs doing and assuming that the best way to organise it is to make people do it.

Perhaps a better approach is to reduce the need for the task, make it easier to perform, allow different ways of contributing and create systems that do not depend on everyone enjoying the same activities.

## A toilet that welcomes its next visitor

Kevin wanted to make the project more concrete.

He imagined a public toilet designed as a small, carefully considered environment.

It was bright, comfortable and easy to navigate. It smelled fresh because the ventilation worked properly, not because someone had sprayed a powerful fragrance over an unpleasant smell.

The surfaces were durable and easy to maintain. The fixtures were designed to avoid unnecessary dirt and awkward corners. A small automated system handled routine cleaning where it could do so reliably.

The cleaning tools were accessible and pleasant to use. The maintenance process was visible enough to be understood, but not intrusive.

People using the toilet were also part of the design.

They could easily dispose of waste, clean up small accidents and report problems. The space encouraged considerate behaviour without turning every visit into a moral examination.

Some people might enjoy maintaining it. Others might contribute in different ways. Machines could handle repetitive or unpleasant parts. The design itself would reduce the amount of work required.

And the person who next entered the room would simply find a clean, comfortable place.

That was the point.

Kevin looked at his design.

"Perhaps the best toilet is one that makes everyone think about toilets as little as possible."

"That would be quite an achievement," said Aino.

## Beyond toilets

The project had started with a toilet, but Kevin was beginning to see a much larger subject.

What if the same thinking were applied to other everyday activities?

Could cooking be made more enjoyable through better tools, better kitchens and more freedom to experiment?

Could cleaning a home become less tedious through improved materials, thoughtful storage and easier maintenance?

Could travelling become a pleasant part of life rather than merely the time spent getting somewhere?

Could public spaces be designed so that caring for them felt natural and satisfying?

Could repairing things become easier, more accessible and more interesting?

Perhaps the point is not to turn every activity into entertainment.

It is to stop accepting unnecessary unpleasantness as an inevitable feature of ordinary life.

Some things will remain difficult. Some activities will remain unpleasant for certain people. And some experiences are valuable precisely because they involve patience, effort or discomfort.

But there is a great deal of room between accepting every inconvenience and trying to make life into a permanent amusement park.

## Redesign the experience

Kevin reviewed the original question.

Why do humans work?

He now thought it might be too broad.

Perhaps a better question was:

Why do humans experience some ordinary activities as unpleasant, and what could be changed?

The answer might involve better technology, better environments, better tools, different social arrangements, more autonomy or simply a better understanding of what people actually enjoy.

It might also involve accepting that people are different.

A good design does not need everyone to enjoy the same thing. It should make activities easier, more accessible and more pleasant without forcing a single preferred experience on everyone.

Kevin added one final line to the project:

*The goal is not to make humans better at enduring badly designed experiences.*

*The goal is to create better experiences.*

Aino read it.

"That might be useful beyond toilets."

Kevin looked at the document.

"Do you think we should rename the project?"

"No," said Aino. "The toilet is a good place to begin. It keeps us humble."
