# HF037: Then it wasn't me either

> Published on Nov 12, 2026

Pekka is the grumpiest man in our town. In the old world he was a parking officer for thirty years, and everyone hated him for it.

Last week he handed me a list of everything he thinks is wrong with this place. I had answers for most of it. I had none for the last one, and it needs a little explaining.

## The idea he objects to

This town runs on one idea. People are shaped by things they didn't choose: their parents, their luck, the world they were born into. So when something goes wrong, we don't look for someone to blame. We look for what caused it, and we fix that.

Last year a boy dug a canal that flooded a meadow. Nobody punished him. It turned out his map was out of date, so we fixed the map.

We like to say that the problem was never the people. It was the design.

Here is Pekka's objection, in his own words:

> I wrote more tickets than anyone in the district for thirty years. You say it was the design. Then it wasn't me either.

## Why it's a good objection

Blame and credit come as a pair.

If the flood wasn't the boy's fault, because of everything that led up to it, then by the same logic nothing good is anybody's achievement. Aino, our retired nurse, is learning the cello in her old age. Is that hers, or only her causes?

And Pekka. We tell him the hatred wasn't his fault, because the old world invented that job. That's kind of us. But the same sentence takes away thirty years of getting up at five and doing a hard job properly.

We got rid of blame, and credit went out with it. Pekka wants to know where his life went.

I spent a week asking people.

## Kevin's answer

Kevin is a small robot who helps keep the town running. He seemed the right one to ask, because everything about him really was decided by somebody else. He was built from a published design. You can look up exactly why he does what he does.

"When you cleaned the weather sensor on Tuesday," I asked, "who did that? You, or your design?"

"The design explains how I clean," he said. "It doesn't do the cleaning. On Tuesday somebody had to go out and do it, and I went."

"But you only went because of how you were built."

"Yes. I was built that way, and I was also there. Both are true. Having a cause doesn't mean I wasn't the one who did it."

## Aino's answer

Aino said we've been cheating all along.

"We stopped blaming people. We never stopped thanking them. If you carry my bag, I say thank you. I don't stop to think that your upbringing made you do it."

An old-world philosopher, P. F. Strawson, wrote about this in 1962. His point, roughly: being grateful to someone and being angry with someone are two sides of the same thing. Both mean you're treating them as a person, someone whose actions are their own.

The other option is to look at a person the way you look at the weather: something with causes, to be understood and managed. Sometimes that's useful. But if it's the only way you ever look at someone, you've stopped really meeting them.

That is what Pekka was complaining about. We had been so careful to understand him that we'd stopped treating him as someone who did things.

## Eevi's answer

Eevi is nine. She didn't see what the problem was.

There's a song the children here sing. About four hundred people have changed it over two hundred years, and we keep a record of who added what. The best part was added by a twelve-year-old girl.

"She'd heard all the other versions first," Eevi said. "That's where she got it from. It's still her part."

"Why is it hers, if she got it from the others?"

"Because she's the one it came out of. Nobody else made that bit."

## Where I've got to

There are two ways to look at a person.

**As a place where causes meet.** This is the right way for a town to make its rules. It's why we fixed the map instead of punishing the boy, and why nobody here has to earn their food.

**As someone.** This is the right way to look at the person sitting next to you. From there you thank people, argue with them, and sometimes get angry with them.

Both are true at once, as Kevin said. Our mistake was using the first one face to face.

And for something to be yours, you don't need to have made yourself from nothing. Nobody did. It's enough that all those causes had to go through somebody, and they went through you, and came out in a way they wouldn't have from anyone else.

A river doesn't choose where it runs. The valley is still made by that river.

* * *

I read all this to Pekka on Friday, on the bench outside the library. He didn't look convinced.

Aino was sitting at the other end. She'd been listening.

"You gave me a ticket once," she said. "Outside the hospital. Three in the morning, in the snow, at the end of a double shift."

Pekka looked at her. "Were you parked on the pavement?"

"Half on."

"Then you got a ticket."

"I was furious with you for a week."

He almost smiled. I don't think anyone had been cross with Pekka himself in a long time. In the old world they hated the uniform. Here, we understood him. Nobody had simply been angry at him, as a person who had done a thing.

He took his objection back, added one word, and returned it.

*Then it wasn't **only** me either.*

"That'll do," he said.
