# HF021: The unfinished list

> Published on Nov 10, 2026

There is a wall in the library that nobody is particularly proud of.

It's covered in pieces of paper.

Some are drawings. Some are plans. Some are detailed descriptions of things people once thought would be rather wonderful.

A floating garden. A public observatory. A railway to the northern forest. A machine that turns fallen leaves into building materials. A rather ambitious proposal for a communal indoor beach.

None of them happened.

At least, not yet.

Eevi was looking at the wall one afternoon when she asked Aino a question.

"Why don't you finish these?"

Aino looked at the wall.

"Somebody might. Or they might not."

"Then why keep them?"

"Because unfinished doesn't mean useless."

## The old obsession with finishing

The old world had a curious relationship with unfinished things.

A project that wasn't completed was a failure. A degree not finished was a waste. A business that closed was a disaster. A career that changed direction needed explaining.

People stayed in jobs they disliked because they'd already spent years getting there. They continued projects nobody wanted because too much money had already been spent. They finished things simply because they'd started them.

There was even a name for one part of it: the sunk cost fallacy.

The more you'd invested in something, the harder it became to walk away, even when walking away was obviously sensible.

Here, we try something different.

Starting something is easy.

Stopping something is easy too.

## The freedom to change your mind

Aino once joined a group building a small greenhouse.

She spent three afternoons planning it, helped choose the location, and found someone who knew about irrigation.

Then she stopped.

She'd realised she wasn't particularly interested in greenhouses.

She wanted to spend her afternoons learning to play the cello.

Nobody complained.

The greenhouse group continued without her. Someone else took over the irrigation plans. The cello lessons started the following week.

There was no resignation letter, no awkward meeting, and no need to explain why she'd changed her mind.

It wasn't a failure.

It was new information.

## What about the work already done?

This is where things become interesting.

In the old system, unfinished projects often disappeared with the people who started them.

Documents were lost. Knowledge stayed in someone's head. A new team started from scratch because nobody could find the previous team's work.

Here, we try to leave things in a state that someone else can understand.

A project has a simple record:

- What were we trying to do?
- What did we discover?
- What worked?
- What didn't?
- What remains to be done?
- Why did we stop?

No elaborate reports. No corporate presentation with a photograph of people pointing at a screen.

Just enough information for the next person.

And if nobody ever picks it up again, that's fine too.

## The difference between giving up and stopping

People sometimes ask whether this makes everyone less determined.

I don't think so.

Determination is useful when something matters and the difficulties are worth overcoming.

But persistence isn't automatically a virtue.

Imagine spending five years building a bridge to somewhere nobody wants to go.

Would another five years make you determined or simply lost?

Sometimes the best contribution is recognising that the original idea was wrong.

Sometimes somebody else has a better idea.

And sometimes the world has changed since you started.

Stopping is not the opposite of progress. Sometimes it's how progress happens.

## The world is one enormous sandbox

There's another place where unfinished things deserve a little more appreciation.

The environment itself.

We have inherited a rather strange idea that every piece of land should eventually become something finished. A park, a building, a farm, a road, a carefully maintained garden. Everything should have a purpose, preferably one that can be explained on a map.

But perhaps the world doesn't need to be finished.

Perhaps some places should simply be places where things happen.

A half-built structure that becomes a shelter for birds. A patch of land where somebody starts growing vegetables and eventually loses interest. An experimental garden that develops into a small forest. A pathway that leads nowhere in particular because someone once thought it might be interesting to walk there.

These things aren't necessarily mistakes.

They are part of how an environment develops.

A place can be useful without being productive. It can be beautiful without being maintained. It can be interesting without being completed.

And sometimes the best thing we can do with a piece of land is leave it alone.

## Designing the important bits

Of course, this doesn't mean abandoning all planning.

Some places work better when things are coordinated.

A larger town, a metropolitan area or an important transport hub needs a certain amount of deliberate design. Water, energy, public transport, housing, healthcare, logistics and shared infrastructure need to work together.

You don't want everyone independently deciding where the sewage pipes should go.

There are benefits to planning these places as coherent systems.

But even here, the planning doesn't need to extend to every square metre or every possible activity.

The infrastructure can be carefully designed while the spaces between it remain open to experimentation.

A city can have a well-organised transport network and still have an empty lot where somebody tries something completely unexpected.

A neighbourhood can have thoughtfully designed public spaces without deciding exactly how every corner must be used.

The important thing is to distinguish between what needs coordination and what benefits from freedom.

A railway station should probably be designed to work.

The little garden next to it doesn't necessarily need a five-year development strategy.

## Nothing needs to justify its existence

Perhaps the most important change is that people no longer have to turn every interest into something useful.

You can start learning pottery without planning to sell pots.

You can build a boat without intending to sail anywhere.

You can spend six months researching mushrooms and then decide you're more interested in birds.

You can start a project, enjoy it, learn something, and stop.

Not everything needs an outcome.

Not everything needs to scale.

Not everything needs to become a contribution to society.

Some things are simply interesting for a while.

And that's enough.

## A small addition

Last week, Eevi added something to the wall.

It was a drawing of a very complicated machine.

There were pipes, wheels, a large red button and something that looked suspiciously like a courgette.

"What does it do?" I asked.

"I don't know yet."

"Are you going to build it?"

"Maybe."

She pinned it between the indoor beach and the leaf machine.

Then she went outside to look at some interesting leaves.

Nobody asked when the machine would be ready.

Nobody asked for a project plan.

The wall had plenty of space.

And, for once, nobody seemed to be in a hurry to fill it.

Perhaps the same should be true of the world outside.

Not every empty space is waiting for a building. Not every unfinished thing is waiting for completion. Not every idea needs to survive its first encounter with reality.

We are allowed to explore, abandon, rethink and start again.

The world is one enormous sandbox.

We might as well enjoy playing in it.
