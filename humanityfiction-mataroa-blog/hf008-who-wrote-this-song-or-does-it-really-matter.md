# HF008: Who wrote this song? Or does it really matter?

> Published on Oct 25, 2026

A song was stuck in my head all morning. I heard it in the park yesterday: kids singing it while they queued for the bouncy bench.

I wanted to know who wrote it, so I looked it up on the Earth-monitor.

The answer: nobody, and about four hundred people.

It started as a work song, sung by people pulling nets somewhere on a coast two hundred years ago. Someone slowed it down into a lullaby. Someone else sped it up again. For a few decades it was the jingle on a bread-oven timer. A choir in another town added harmonies. A twelve-year-old added the middle part that everyone now thinks is the best bit. And last week, apparently, the courgette man added a verse about courgettes.

There are 412 versions. Anyone can listen to all of them, and anyone can make the 413th.

One of them, the choir's, carries a small note: *please credit, please don't change.* Nobody has. The tree just grows around it.

## One big creative person

The old world told art as a story about lone geniuses. One person, one idea, one name on the cover.

It was never really like that. Every song is built from older songs. Shakespeare borrowed most of his plots. Many folk songs had no author at all, just singers who changed them a little each time. Every artwork, like every invention, is assembled from centuries of inherited work.

Here, we just stopped pretending otherwise. Culture works like one huge creative person: humanity humming to itself, trying an idea, dropping it, picking it up again a century later, changing a line. Each of us is a passing thought in that big head. Some thoughts are better than others. All of them belong to the same mind.

Not everyone sees it this way. Some people feel a song is a piece of them. That's allowed too. More on that below.

## So what's credit for?

Mostly for finding more.

If I love the middle part of that song, I want to know where it came from. Then I can hear what else that twelve-year-old made, or what she was listening to when she made it. Credit isn't a price tag or a fence. It's a map.

So every piece of work carries its family tree: what it came from, and what came from it. Not to pay anyone. Just so you can follow the thread.

Names are optional. Plenty of people never add theirs. Their versions are still on the tree, signed "someone".

Others do want their name to travel with the work. That's the "BY" in the old Creative Commons licences: please credit me. Nobody can force it, but most people are happy to do it.

## Remix anything

Anything shared can be copied, changed, and shared again. Every film, every song, every book.

The original doesn't disappear when someone remixes it. Copies live on thousands of devices, so the first version stays exactly as it was, right next to all the new ones. Like forks in open source, or a second bench next to the first.

But not everyone wants their work changed. For them, there are soft licences, borrowed from the old Creative Commons ones. "BY-ND", for example, means: please credit me, and please don't change this.

It's a request, not a lock. Nothing stops anyone from changing the work anyway. But politeness is highly respected here. When nothing forces you to be considerate, being considerate is exactly what people notice. So most people simply honour the request. Some make their version and keep it to themselves. A few ask the maker first, and sometimes get a surprising yes.

## The same three rules

You can remix anything. You still can't harm, deceive, or block.

- **Turning a sad song into a funny one:** fine. That's taste.
- **Making a song that mocks a real person's grief:** that's harm.
- **Claiming you wrote something you didn't:** that's deceit. Forgetting where something came from is fine. Lying about it isn't.
- **Hiding the original so only your version exists:** that's blocking.
- **Changing a work whose maker politely asked you not to:** none of the three. It's allowed. It's just rude, and people notice.

## Isn't rudeness other people's problem?

Maybe. Mostly.

When someone changes my song after I asked them not to, something in me flinches. But what exactly got hurt? Not the song. The original is still there, untouched. What got hurt is an expectation: I thought my request would matter to them, and it didn't.

That feeling is real. But expectations are mine. I made them, and I carry them. Nobody else promised to carry them for me.

In the old world, a lot of politeness wasn't really politeness. It was fear: of the boss, the customer, the neighbours' opinions. People smiled because they had to, and everyone half-knew it.

Here, nobody has to. So when someone is considerate, it means something. It's a small gift, freely given. Seen this way, rudeness is just a gift not given. You can wish for a gift, but you can't demand one. A demanded gift becomes a bill. Remember tipping.

Maybe that's why politeness is so admired here. Not because it's required, but because it never is.

And yet rudeness is admired too, sometimes.

Every culture has hidden rules: things nobody wrote down, but everybody obeys. Someone has to break them, or nobody would ever notice they were there. The court jester. The punk. The artist who signed a urinal and entered it into an art show. They were rude, and some of them ended up among the most respected people of their time.

Here, the hidden-rule-breakers have their fans too. Changing the choir's version against their wish is rude. Doing it so cleverly that everyone hears the original in a new way is still rude, but it's also something else: a gift to everyone, except maybe the choir.

So maybe "was that rude?" is the wrong question. A better one: did it actually hurt anyone? And did it leave us, as a whole, richer or poorer?

Rudeness withholds something: attention, care, a little respect. Harm takes something: someone's safety, their dignity, their place among us. A rude remix that annoys one choir but sets a hundred people writing new songs leaves the whole richer. Rudeness that makes people stop sharing leaves it poorer. The big creative person loses some of its thoughts.

It's also worth remembering that both people are carrying something. The rude one might be having the worst week of their life. The offended one might be carrying expectations nobody agreed to. And manners differ from town to town. One person's directness is another person's insult.

If culture really is one big creative person, then rudeness is that person being a bit short with itself. Every mind has a few impatient voices. They're part of how thinking works, and sometimes they're the ones with the new idea. The point isn't to silence them. It's just not to let them take over.

## What actually matters

Something doesn't have to be dangerous to matter. Loneliness matters. A kid's first song matters. Someone's grief matters.

But most of what people get worked up about doesn't: who changed which verse, what colour the bench is, whether someone said thank you in exactly the right way.

A big part of a good life, and maybe of a good society, is a cheerful *"who gives a shit?"* about all that. Not cold, not careless. Just light. Save your caring for the things that deserve it, and let the rest blow away.

If this world has pillars, that's one of them.

## Does anyone get famous?

Sometimes. People in the park recognize the twelve-year-old now, and she likes that.

But fame here is lighter. You can't turn it into money, a big house, or power over others. It's just a lot of people being glad you exist. Which, honestly, was always the good part of fame.

The best reputations often don't go to the most talented people anyway. They go to the most considerate ones: the people who credit others and respect polite requests.

And nobody has to sell a song to eat. Everyone already has enough. So people make music for the reasons people always secretly made music: because they wanted to, and because someone else might like it.

## Meanwhile, in the old world

For decades, a company collected fees whenever "Happy Birthday" was used in films and on TV. Then, in 2015, a court decided the company had no valid copyright on the lyrics. One of the most-sung songs in the world, and people were still arguing about who owned it.

Here, nobody owns it. Everyone sings it a bit badly, which is the correct way.

* * *

I hummed the song all the way home. I got the middle part wrong.

Version 413.
