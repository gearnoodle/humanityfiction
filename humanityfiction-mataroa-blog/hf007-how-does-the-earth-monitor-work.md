# HF007: How does the Earth-monitor work?

> Published on Oct 25, 2026

Everyone in town uses it. Nobody seems to know how it works.

Aino asked me about it while checking my stitches. ("It's not red," I said, before she could ask.) She wanted to know who decides what shows up on her page. I said nobody. She didn't believe me. I wasn't sure I did either.

So I went to find the people who keep it running. Or, more precisely, the people who felt like keeping it running this week.

I found them in the library basement: Tuula, a retired postwoman who handles reports of wrong data. Sami, who used to work at a bank and now looks after how things get sorted. And Kevin, the drain robot, who cleans sensors on his days off.

## Where does the data come from?

Three places.

**Sensors.** Water quality, air, energy, rainfall, how full the shared cars are, how many people sat on a bench today.

**People.** Issue reports, updates like M.'s note about the bench, Hilkka's paper note about the pike. Anyone can add anything.

**Machines that connect the dots.** They spot patterns nobody asked about, like an unusual number of people waking at 4:17.

Every number carries its own little family tree, like the songs do: where it came from, who or what measured it, and when. "If you can't see where a number came from," Tuula said, "don't trust it."

## Who built it?

Nobody, all at once. It grew out of lots of small projects in the old world.

The old world already had pieces of it: a map of the whole world drawn by volunteers, called OpenStreetMap, and thousands of homemade air-quality sensors on people's balconies. Towns started sharing dashboards. Someone joined them together. Then someone else improved that.

There's no central computer. Everyone's devices store a tiny slice of it, so every piece exists in thousands of places at once. There's nothing to switch off, and nothing for anyone to own.

## Who fixes it when it breaks?

I asked them to please not say "anyone."

"Fine," Sami said. "Specific people. Just different ones every week."

Each part has its own crew: sensors, maps, translations, sorting. Each crew has the usual sign-up lists, waitlists and waitlists for the waitlists. This week, the sorting crew is Sami and four people he's never met.

## What happens when the data is wrong?

Every number has a "this looks wrong" button. Anyone can press it.

Sensors also check each other. When one weather station got warmer while the ones around it didn't, the Earth-monitor flagged it as odd. Tuula walked over to look, and found the cat.

The cat now has its own entry:

> **Cat, weather station 14** · Status: asleep

Mistakes stay visible, and so do the corrections. And because data comes from so many sources at once, faking a need is very hard. One false report looks odd next to fifty true ones.

## Counting, not watching

This was the question I cared about most.

The rule is simple: sensors count, they never identify. The bench sensor knows three people sat on it today. It has no idea who.

And your page lives on your own device. It learns what you know, where you live and what catches your curiosity, but all of that stays with you. Nothing about you is sent anywhere else. "Your page knows you," Sami said. "The Earth-monitor doesn't."

## Can you fork it?

Yes. The data is shared, but anyone can build their own way of looking at it.

Some towns sort by "most neglected first." There's a slow version that only updates once a week, for people who find the full one too much. Hilkka might have liked that one. Different versions get compared, and good ideas spread.

## What it never does

It never says "go." No streaks, no points, no rewards, no red badges begging for attention.

And the page has an end. After the day's needs, offers and three small tasks, it simply says: *That's all for today.*

In the old world, screens were designed to keep you looking. This one is designed to let you go.

## And why my page shows courgettes every single day?

Sami looked it up.

In July, I clicked on a courgette soup recipe. Once. My page decided it was a lifelong passion.

* * *

I pressed "less of this."

The next morning, my page suggested a recipe for marrows.
