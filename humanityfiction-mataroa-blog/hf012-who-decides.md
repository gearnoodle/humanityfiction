# HF012: Who decides?

> Published on Nov 1, 2026

The kids never finished their second bench by the library. Instead, they picked a new spot: the meadow behind it.

The next morning, a proposal appeared on the Earth-monitor:

> **Meadow behind the library** · Proposal: no benches here · Reason: ground-nesting birds, April to July

It was signed "someone speaking for the birds."

So who gets to decide that? If nobody is in charge of a meadow, who's in charge of a railway, a river, or the air? And with millions of things to do, who decides what comes first?

## Two questions

Every decision here gets measured with two simple questions:

1. **How many people does it touch?**
2. **How hard is it to undo?**

A cushion on a bench: few people, easy to undo. Just do it. A dam on a river: thousands of people, almost impossible to undo. That one gets the slowest, widest, most careful process there is.

There's an old name for the idea behind this: subsidiarity. Decide as close to the bench as possible, and only go bigger when the decision really is bigger.

## Small: the meadow

Nobody declares a no-bench zone. Someone proposes one, and says why.

Then anyone affected can object, but only with real reasons: it harms, it deceives, or it blocks. "I really wanted a bench there" isn't one. "The path to the lake goes through it" might be.

If nobody has a serious objection for a while, the proposal holds. Not forever, just until someone has a better idea. The question isn't "does everyone love this?" It's "is it good enough for now, and safe enough to try?"

The kids grumbled, then built their bench next to the meadow instead. It faces the birds.

## Medium: the town

For bigger things, like a new library or moving a road, a proposal isn't enough. Somebody has to really dig in, and most people don't have the time.

So the town picks a small group by lottery. Like a jury, but for decisions. Ancient Athens chose many of its officials this way, and in the old world, Ireland used randomly chosen citizens' assemblies for some of its hardest questions.

A lottery has nice side effects. Nobody campaigns. Nobody keeps power. Anyone might be picked, and anyone can say no thanks. The group works for a few weeks, publishes its reasoning, and dissolves. Everyone else can comment along the way.

## Large: forty towns, one river

When a decision touches many towns, like a railway or a river, each town sends one or two people to a shared panel. They're also picked by lottery, and they carry their town's views, not its power.

Experts advise. They don't decide.

Big projects get broken into small, reversible steps, and new ideas are tried in one place first. If two towns disagree, they can often each try their own way. But some things can't be forked. There is only one river, and only one sky. Those get the most care of all.

One more rule of thumb: if a project makes life a little better for many people but a lot worse for a few, those few aren't just outvoted. That's harm. Either it gets made right for them, or the project finds another way.

Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize for showing that communities can run shared things like forests and fisheries themselves, when the people affected make the rules together. This is that idea, all the way up.

## Millions of projects, no master list

At any moment there are millions of open projects, from one wobbly bench to the whole sky. Nobody ranks them all. Nobody could.

The old world didn't either. Prices were a rough signal that let millions of people decide for themselves. The Earth-monitor does the same job with better signals. Every issue is scored on a few simple dials: how urgent, how bad, how many people it touches, how hard it is to undo, and who nearby could help. Mix a few dials and you get endless priorities, without anyone writing a list.

Then every person's page sorts things for them, by their skills, their street and their curiosity. Billions of small personal sorts add up to a planet sorting itself.

Big projects break down into small pieces. Nobody "saves the river". People sort a few photos of river insects, plant ten willows, or check one water sample.

There are two traps. Fun things get swarmed while dull, important things get ignored, so the Earth-monitor also shows what's most neglected for its size. And whoever designs the dials quietly holds a lot of power, so the dials themselves are open: argued over, forked, and tested town by town.

## Today's three bites

Every morning, my page shows three small tasks: one from a small project, one from a medium one, and one from a large one.

Today:

- **Small:** water the tomatoes outside the library. Ten minutes.
- **Medium:** check one page of the town's new cycling map. Twenty minutes.
- **Large:** sort five photos of river insects for the forty-town river survey. Fifteen minutes.

Hardly anyone wants to join big projects one after another. But anyone can take one bite of something huge on a Tuesday, then get on with their life. And a small, light task before breakfast does wonders for the mood.

## Alarms and armies

The Earth-monitor works a bit like a body. Most signals are local, and most problems are handled where they happen. But a few signals are alarms, like pain or fever, and they override everything.

When something is urgent, process waits. Whoever is closest acts, and explains afterwards. If a town can't cope, the alarm spreads to its neighbours, then further, until there are enough hands. Any temporary role that comes with an emergency ends automatically when the emergency does. Power that expires by default.

And there's something a body doesn't have: help that arrives before the disaster.

People jokingly call them disaster armies: engineers, builders, planners, cooks and helpers of every kind, who travel to wherever trouble is likely and get it ready. In earthquake zones, they rebuild ordinary houses into earthquake-proof ones. Along rivers, they raise flood walls. In dry forests, they cut firebreaks. Nobody orders them. Many just love building things in new places with good people.

In the old world, an earthquake of the same size could kill thousands in one country and very few in another, mostly depending on how the buildings were made. Here, people still live wherever they like, even on fault lines. The armies just make sure that wherever they choose is as safe as it can be.

## So who's in charge?

Of the big projects, a coordinator. Or a few.

In the old world, the person in charge of a big project had power. Here, they have a to-do list. They keep track of who's doing what and what's still open. They rotate, anyone can see what they do, and they can be replaced any time.

* * *

Last week, I got a message: I'd been randomly picked for the panel on the new railway between forty towns.

I said yes. The first item on the agenda was where to put the benches at the stations.

Of course it was.
