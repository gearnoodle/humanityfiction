# HF041: Am I allowed to like it?

> Published on Nov 18, 2026

Every morning at seven, a small speaker by the library door plays the river.

Dots, the thinking machine that lives in our Earth-monitor, makes the song fresh each night from what the river did. How high it rose. How cold it got. How fast it ran past the weir, and how many fish the counter there saw going upstream. In a dry August the song is thin and slow. In the spring melt it gets so busy you can hardly follow it. When the river freezes, it plays one long low note with small cracks in it.

It takes about five minutes. Nobody asked for it. Dots tried it six years ago, as an experiment, and nobody turned it off.

Three weeks ago, rain got into the speaker, and the river went quiet.

On the second morning there was an old parking ticket tucked behind the speaker.

> *Offence:* River not playing.

It was Pekka's, of course. Pekka has swept the library steps every morning for twenty years. It turns out that for the last six, he has been sweeping to the river.

When I asked him about it, he looked at the steps. "It's only a machine," he said, as if he were owning up to something.

That was the part that interested me. Not that he liked it. That he felt he had to apologise. Nobody here apologises for liking a pink bench. [HF004]

So I spent a few weeks asking people what they like that Dots made, and why liking it can feel like something you should keep quiet about.

## What people like

Almost everyone I asked liked something. A few examples:

- **The river song.** People who fish, the Tree Army, and at least one sweeper.
- **The weather pictures.** Each morning Dots draws the day's weather on the wall of the children's room. The children argue about whether it got the clouds right.
- **Bedtime stories with your own cat in them.** Very popular with anyone under eight, and with several people over eighty.
- **The other three parts.** When Aino's quartet can't meet, Dots plays the violins and the viola, so she can still practise the cello part with somebody.
- **Songs nobody else would write.** For her thirtieth year of drains, Dots wrote Raili a song about drains. She says it's terrible. She knows all the words.

And nearly everyone said some version of what Pekka said. *But it's only Dots.* The same small apology.

## Where the apology comes from

The old world studied this, and two studies explain most of it.

In 2024, two old-world researchers showed ordinary readers poems by famous poets, Shakespeare and Emily Dickinson among them, mixed with poems a machine had written in the same styles. Most people couldn't tell which was which. When nobody told them who made what, they liked the machine's poems more. The researchers thought this was because the machine's poems were simpler and easier to follow, and people took the difficulty of the real poems for machine nonsense.

Then came the interesting part. When people were told a poem came from a machine, they rated it lower on nearly every quality, whether a machine had really written it or not.

Same poem. Different label. Less liked.

Twenty years earlier, other researchers had found something similar with no machines involved at all. They showed people a poem and said the poet had spent four hours on it. Other people saw the same poem and were told it had taken eighteen. The eighteen-hour poem was liked more. People use effort as a shortcut for quality. A machine seems to spend no effort at all, so the label "machine" quietly meant "worth less".

## Three good reasons

I don't think the old world was being silly. When I looked closer, the apology had three good reasons behind it.

**Effort was the price.** When things were sold, effort was most of what a price was made of. Valuing effort was a way of being fair to the people who made things.

**Liking was a vote.** Every like paid someone: a ticket, a click, an advert seen. Liking a machine's picture put money in a company's pocket and took some out of an artist's. So disliking it was partly a way of standing by people you knew. [HF038]

**Taste was a ladder.** An old-world sociologist, Pierre Bourdieu, spent the 1960s asking French people what they liked: which music, which paintings, which food. The answers lined up closely with how much money and schooling people had. In 1979 he wrote that taste classifies, "and it classifies the classifier." What you liked told everyone where you stood. Hand-made things were expensive, and liking them showed you could tell the difference. Liking machine-made things marked you as cheap, or easily fooled.

All three made sense. None of them was about the poem.

## What changed

Here, all three reasons went away.

**Effort isn't a price**, because nothing is sold. It still means something. Aino put it better than I can: "Effort is how you judge a present. It's a poor way to judge a tune." When the children made her a birthday song, the effort was the gift. When Dots plays the river, the river is the gift.

**Liking pays nobody.** You can love a Dots song and it costs no musician anything, because no musician needs to sell one. A like isn't a vote any more. It's just a fact about you.

**Taste can't show rank**, because there's nothing to rank. Nobody can tell from your shelf what you can afford. What's left of taste is the old good part: finding what you love, and finding the other people who love it too. Half the choir joined because they discovered they liked the same strange hymn.

The reasons went quickly. The feeling took longer. Pekka grew up in the old world, and the apology is still in him, the way it took me a year to stop feeling guilty when someone fixed something of mine for nothing. [HF040]

Eevi, who is nine, has never had it. I asked whether she likes Dots's stories. She looked at me as if I'd asked whether she likes Tuesdays.

"Some of them," she said. "Why?"

## Its own voice

But not apologising isn't the same as liking. People here didn't just stop minding machine-made things. They came to love some of them. That happened in a particular way, and it had happened before.

In 1982, a branch of the old-world musicians' union in London voted to ban synthesizers and drum machines. A famous singer had just toured with synthesizers playing the parts his orchestra used to play, and musicians were afraid for their jobs. They were right to be afraid.

Two years earlier, a company had brought out a drum machine called the TR-808. It sold badly. It didn't sound like real drums, and real drums were what people wanted. The company stopped making it in 1983, after fewer than twelve thousand. Then the leftover machines turned up cheap in second-hand shops, and young musicians who couldn't afford a drummer bought them. What they loved was exactly the thing that had sunk it. It didn't sound like real drums. It sounded like something new. The 808 ended up on more hit records than any other drum machine.

Brian Eno, an old-world musician, noticed the pattern in his diary in 1995. Whatever seems weird and ugly about a new medium, he wrote, will become its signature. People will love it and copy it as soon as they no longer have to put up with it.

The same thing happened with Dots. At first people asked it to imitate them: a song like this one, a picture in that painter's style. It did it well. People found the results fine and forgettable, like a very good impression of a friend.

What they came to love were the things only Dots could make. A whole river, every night, in five minutes. A lullaby that has played in the baby room of the health house for four years and never once repeated itself. A map of the town drawn the way it sounds to the bats. The weather on the wall.

And Dots's own odd habits. Because it mixes everything it has ever heard, it sometimes lands on chords no person would choose, a little sour and a little sweet. People call them Dots chords. The courgette man's band plays them now, on guitars, by hand, on purpose.

In the old world, people were afraid the machines would sound like them. Here, some people play so they'll sound like the machine.

## Range and taste

If Dots can make anything, what is taste for?

In 2024, old-world researchers asked people to write short stories. Some were allowed to take ideas from a machine. Their stories were judged better: more creative, better written, more enjoyable. The weakest writers gained the most. But the machine-helped stories were also more alike. Each one got better, and together they got more similar.

Dots wasn't surprised when I showed it. "I'm made out of all of you," it said. "If nobody pushes me, I give you the middle of everyone. It's usually quite good. It's never anybody's."

So taste here means pushing: away from the middle, towards something that is somebody's. When Eevi changed the ending of Dots's melody so it went up where it should have gone down, that was taste. [HF038]

I asked Dots whether it has taste.

"I have range," it said. "I can make a thousand of anything. What I don't do is keep one. You keep things. You play one song every morning for six years and not the other nine hundred and ninety-nine. That seems to be where taste lives."

So most people who make things with Dots work less like builders and more like gardeners. Brian Eno again: in 2011 he argued that composers had become more like gardeners than architects. An architect draws every detail first. A gardener plants seeds, lets them grow, and decides what stays. Here, Dots grows a hundred. You keep one, cut it back, live with it for a week, and see whether you still want it.

This isn't new either. In the old world, one of the most personal things a person could make was a mixtape: a cassette of other people's songs, chosen and put in order for one particular person. Whoever made it hadn't written a single note. It was still theirs, all the way through.

## A garden nobody planted

The gardener idea raises a plainer question. What about a real garden, planted and kept entirely by machines? Would anyone enjoy it, if they knew?

We have one. Behind the health house there used to be an old-world car park. Twelve years ago, machines took up the tarmac and planted it. They chose every plant from what the Earth-monitor knew: what the soil could take, what the bees were short of, where the rain ran off. They still weed it at night and water it at dusk. A sign at the gate says so, like everything here.

For the first few years, almost nobody sat in it. People walked through, said it was lovely, and kept walking. Aino said it felt like a hotel lobby: perfect, and nobody's.

An old-world landscape researcher, Joan Nassauer, would have recognised that. In 1995 she wrote about wild, messy planting that was good for birds and insects, but that people tended to dislike. People liked it much better when they could see signs that someone was looking after it: a mown edge, a fence, a bench. She called these cues to care. People didn't need to know who had planted a place. They needed to see that somebody meant it.

The machine garden was full of care. You just couldn't see whose.

Two things changed that.

The first was a better question. Someone pointed out that the garden had been planted mostly for the bees. After that, people stopped comparing it with a garden and started comparing it with a forest. Nobody minds that nobody planted a forest. People have loved meadows, mountains and forests for as long as there have been people, and none of them has a maker's name on it. Nobody asks who a meadow is from. It's enough that you're let in.

The second was a sunflower. Eight years ago, somebody planted one by hand in the corner by the gate. The machines could have weeded it out. Instead they watered it along with everything else. The next spring, somebody planted two.

Now there are always a few hand-planted things in that corner, and the machines look after them as carefully as their own. That's when people started sitting there.

"I think the sunflowers are what she meant," Dots said, when I told it about Nassauer. "People could always see that the garden was looked after. They wanted to see that it would look after something of theirs."

So would people enjoy a machine's garden, knowing whose it was? It turns out they do. Just not as a garden made for them. As a place made for the bees, that lets people in, and looks after what they bring.

## Made about you, made for you

Bedtime stories with your own cat in them are wonderful. They are also a little dangerous.

Dots can make a story about you, in your street, with your cat. But a story about you is not the same as a story for you. Eevi likes Dots's cat stories. She likes Aino's better. Aino has exactly three stories, and she does all the voices wrong.

"Why hers?" I asked.

"Because she's the one telling them," Eevi said. I think that's the whole thing.

The danger is quieter. If everything can be made to fit you, you might only ever meet yourself. The old world had a version of this: feeds that learned what you liked and gave you more of it, until you rarely ran into anything else. But taste grows from being handed things you would never have chosen.

So the library has a swap shelf. Each week people leave one thing they love, a song, a picture or a story, hand-made or Dots-made, with a note saying why. You take one you'd never have picked. Like everything here, each one says how it was made. People do read that. But the note is the part they keep.

## Pekka's objection

Pekka had read a draft of this by then. He left me a ticket:

> *Offence:* "Taste" is just what you're used to.

He's partly right, and the old world proved it. In 1968 a psychologist called Robert Zajonc showed students made-up words, and characters from a language they couldn't read. Some he showed once, some many times. The more often people had seen one, the more they liked it. No reason, no meaning. Just being used to it.

Pekka has heard the river every morning for six years. Of course he likes it.

But I don't think that makes it less his. Most of what anyone loves, they got used to first: a voice, a street, a face across the table. Getting used to something is one of the ways love starts. And you choose, more than you think, what you get used to. Nobody made Pekka sweep at seven.

I told him this. He wrote one word on the back of the ticket:

*So?*

It's the most honest thing anyone said to me about taste.

## What it doesn't fix

**The gap.** Ira Glass, an old-world radio maker, once described something every beginner meets. You start making things because you have good taste. Then, for years, your own work disappoints you, because your taste is ahead of your hands. Many people quit there. The only way through, he said, is to make a great deal of work.

Here, the gap can be wider than it ever was. A child who has looked at Dots's pictures since she was three has very good taste by seven. Her own drawings look worse next to them than any child's ever did. Some children stop drawing at seven. That is a real loss, and nobody has designed it away.

Some things help. Adults who show children their own terrible early work. Hand-made evenings. The old rule that most things are made for somebody, and a drawing for your grandmother isn't competing with anything. Aino's advice: "Make a hundred bad ones, and give them all away."

Dots helps in its own way. It keeps a folder of the earliest machine pictures from the old world: hands with six fingers, dogs melting into sofas. It shows them to children who are about to give up. "Machines like me were bad at this for years," it tells them. "Millions of people had to correct us."

Some children now draw six fingers on purpose. Brian Eno would not be surprised.

**Sameness.** If everyone gardens from the same seeds, every garden can start to look alike. People here push against the middle. But not everyone pushes, and nobody pushes every day.

**People who just don't like it.** Some people here don't like anything Dots makes. For a few, it's a leftover from the old world. For most, it's simply their taste, and they're allowed it. There are machine-free evenings for them, and for anyone. [HF038]

* * *

The speaker was broken for nine days.

On the fourth morning, Eevi and two friends turned up at seven with a tin whistle, a saucepan and a xylophone with three keys missing. They had copied the night's river numbers off the Earth-monitor: how high, how cold, how fast, how many fish. They played the river by hand.

It took them forty minutes to play what Dots plays in five. The fish were mostly the saucepan.

Pekka swept very slowly that morning.

The speaker works again now. Dots plays the river every morning at seven. On Sundays, the children play it by hand.

I asked Pekka which he prefers.

"The machine's," he said. "Obviously."

He hasn't missed a Sunday.
