# HF025: The things the design can't fix

> Published on Nov 11, 2026

Kevin had finished his notes on the good life and the pleasant toilet. He had one more question, and he was almost reluctant to ask it.

"If the floor is solid," he said to Aino, "and nobody is trapped, and almost everything that used to hurt people has been redesigned… why do some people still look unhappy?"

Aino was re-potting a small fern on the library windowsill. She didn't look up.

"Because the design only ever claimed to fix the unnecessary suffering," she said. "The necessary kind was always going to stay."

## The honest limit

The Earth-monitor can show a blocked drain, a lonely person who has asked for company, a meadow that needs protecting, or a skill that someone is willing to share. It can make the next small helpful action visible and easy.

It cannot make a person stop missing someone who has died.  
It cannot make a shy person suddenly find social ease.  
It cannot erase a childhood that left someone expecting the world to be dangerous.  
It cannot give someone a sense of purpose if they have not yet found one.  
It cannot force two people to love each other, or even to like each other.  
It cannot prevent the ordinary human experience of waking up some mornings and feeling that nothing in particular is worth getting up for.

The design was never meant to produce permanent contentment. It was meant to stop people from suffering for reasons that were, in the end, optional.

Once those optional reasons were gone, what remained was simply being human.

## The residual ache

Some people in town still carry a quiet restlessness.

They have food, a place to sleep, time, and no one demanding anything of them. Yet they sometimes walk the long way around the pink bench, or sit on it without speaking, or stare at the Earth-monitor without choosing any of the three small tasks.

Kevin found this inefficient.

"If their needs are met," he said, "the feeling should resolve."

"Needs are not the same as longings," Aino replied. "And longings are not always problems to be solved."

A person can have enough and still want something that cannot be handed to them: a particular kind of recognition, a lost version of themselves, a relationship that ended, a talent that never quite arrived, or simply a clearer sense of why they are here.

The old world often treated this ache as a personal failure or a market opportunity. It sold people solutions. The new world mostly leaves it alone.

That is harder than it sounds. It is tempting to fill every empty feeling with another activity, another connection, another project. The Earth-monitor could easily be redesigned to nudge people toward meaning the way the old feeds nudged them toward consumption. It does not. That restraint is deliberate.

Some emptiness is not a bug. It is the space in which a person eventually notices what they actually care about.

## The ones who feel they should be happier

There is a particular discomfort that appears only after the floor is solid.

In the old world, unhappiness had obvious external causes. You could point to the job, the rent, the lack of time, the fear of the bill. Once those causes are removed, the remaining unhappiness has nowhere convenient to sit. It feels more personal. More like a failing.

A few people quietly believe they are doing the good life wrong. They see others absorbed in projects, friendships, or long quiet contentment, and they conclude that they themselves must be missing some essential capacity.

The design tries not to reinforce this.

It does not celebrate the most active people as more successful. It does not rank contribution. It does not turn rest into a productivity technique. It does not suggest that a mediocre Tuesday is a wasted Tuesday.

Still, the feeling arrives anyway. Humans compare. Humans invent private standards and then fall short of them.

Aino calls this the last inherited habit: the belief that a life must justify itself.

## What still helps

The design cannot remove the ache, but it can refuse to make it worse.

It keeps the cost of trying something new extremely low. You can join a project for an afternoon and leave without explanation. You can move house. You can change your name on the monitor. You can disappear for a season and return without a story.

It keeps the cost of doing nothing equally low. Nobody is watching your output. Nobody is waiting for your potential to be fulfilled.

It keeps other people available without making them compulsory. The Loneliness Army exists. So do the benches. So does the simple ability to sit near someone without having to perform interest.

And it keeps the record honest. When someone is struggling, the struggle is allowed to be visible if they choose. There is no pressure to reframe it as growth or resilience or a beautiful journey. Sometimes it is just hard, and the hardest part is that nothing external is wrong.

## Kevin's uncomfortable conclusion

Kevin processed this for a long time.

"Then the system is incomplete," he said.

"Yes," said Aino.

"That is usually considered a flaw."

"In most systems, yes. Here it is the point. A design that claimed to complete the human condition would be lying. Or it would have to start editing the humans."

Kevin considered the second option and discarded it.

"So what is the correct response to the residual ache?"

Aino finished with the fern and wiped her hands.

"The same response as to everything else that cannot be fixed. Make sure it is not made worse by the surroundings. Leave room for it. And stay nearby in case the person wants company while they have it."

## A small observation

That afternoon Eevi sat on the pink bench with her notebook. She was drawing something complicated again. After a while she looked up and said:

"Aino?"

"Yes?"

"Is it okay if I don't know what I'm for yet?"

Aino sat down beside her.

"Yes," she said. "It's more than okay. It's the usual condition."

Eevi thought about this. Then she nodded, closed the notebook, and went to look for interesting leaves.

Kevin logged the exchange under a new heading:

*Some questions are not problems. They are simply the shape a free life makes when it is still deciding.*

He was not sure the entry was efficient. He left it anyway.
