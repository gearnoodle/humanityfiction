# HF029: What was already there

> Published on Nov 11, 2026

Eevi's learning group had a new question for the week: *Was anyone happy in the old world?*

They asked me, because I once owned a wallet.

"Yes," I said. "Quite often."

Eevi looked doubtful. "With the bills? And the jobs? And the bike locks?"

"With all of it. Not all day. But most days, for a bit."

She thought about that. "That's the same as now."

It is. That's the part nobody tells you about either world.

So the group decided to write letters to the old world, to be pinned up in the museum next to the photo of the woman on the crossing. Nobody could say how the letters would get there. They wrote them anyway.

I tried one too. This is roughly what came out.

## What you already have

A surprising amount of this town needed no redesign at all. It was lying around in 2026, free, and mostly unnoticed because everyone was in a hurry.

- Trees. They were never behind a paywall.
- A bench, and someone to sit on it with. Talking optional.
- A long lunch.
- Fixing one small thing for someone who'll never know it was you.
- Singing badly at birthdays.
- Someone who'd notice if you went missing.
- Interesting leaves.

One of your own philosophers, Epicurus, worked this out about 2,300 years ago. He lived in a garden with his friends, ate mostly bread, and counted a little cheese as a feast. He thought that was most of what a good life needed.

People have been rediscovering his garden ever since, and then forgetting where they put it.

## What you can borrow

A few of our habits travel well. None of them needs an Earth-monitor.

**A day that ends.** Our page says *That's all for today* and stops. Yours never did, so you'll have to say it yourself. Out loud helps.

**Three small things.** Not thirty. After that, the rest of the day is allowed to be empty. Empty is where the good accidents get in.

**The tilted bench.** The pink bench leans two millimetres to the left. Kevin, our drain robot, once worked out exactly how to fix it, and then didn't. "Not perfect" and "broken" are different things. Most of a life is the first one.

**Two shrugs.** One for the things you were told to want. One for everybody telling you to be better. The second is harder, and worth more.

**Causes, not faults.** When a day goes badly, ask what led to it. It's usually sleep, food, or something somebody said years ago.

**A mediocre Tuesday.** Fully allowed. We have them constantly.

**Not knowing what you're for.** Eevi asked Aino about this. Aino said it's the usual condition. She's been deciding for a very long time, and seems in no hurry.

This isn't advice, by the way. We shrugged at advice a long time ago. It's just a list of things that turned out to be lying around.

## What you can't borrow

The floor.

I won't pretend otherwise. If what's wrong is the rent, a shrug won't pay it. And if you're tired, it may be because your world asks too much of you, and not because you're doing life badly.

That's the one thing I'd most like you to hear. Where it's hard for you, it's mostly the design.

Your bench is tilted a lot more than two millimetres, and you're still sitting on it. That takes more skill than anything we do here.

## What it's like here, honestly

This might help more than the rest.

We aren't happy all the time either.

The day our Earth-monitor went empty, people cheered. By Thursday they were restless. Your own psychologists had already measured this: people are bad at guessing how good a better future will feel. They guess too high, and for too long.

So people here still get restless. They miss someone. They stare at their three small tasks and pick none of them. Aino says the design only ever fixed the unnecessary suffering. The ordinary kind came with us.

Which means the feeling you might imagine waiting at the end, the one where everything is finally all right, isn't here either. It was never at the end of anything. It turns up for a few minutes at a time, on a bench, in whatever world you happen to have.

You've probably had it this week, and not counted it.

* * *

The letters went up on the museum wall on Friday.

Most were long. One explained the whole Earth-monitor, with diagrams. One apologised for something, though nobody could work out what.

Mine ended: *You're doing fine. Tell us if it goes red.*

Eevi's was one line, in green pencil:

*The leaves were already free. Did you look?*
