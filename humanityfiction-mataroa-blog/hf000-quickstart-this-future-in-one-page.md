# HF000: QUICKSTART: This future in one page

> Published on Oct 24, 2026

This post is meant for those who don't have time to read all the rest. Here it goes:

Same humans, same planet. One thing has changed: the way people live together was designed on purpose.

**The one idea: change the setup, not the people.**
The old world told people to be kinder, greener and harder-working. But it was set up so that doing the opposite paid better. This world changes the setup. Being decent is easy here for a simple reason: you gain almost nothing by being the opposite.

**So is there no crime?**
There is much less, because most of the reasons for it are gone. Think about why people used to break the rules:

- **Need.** Nobody steals food or a place to sleep, because everyone already has them.
- **Greed.** You can't sell a stolen thing, because nothing is sold. There is no money to collect, hide, or leave to your children.
- **Cheating.** People mostly lied to sell something or to avoid paying. Here nothing is sold, and the records are open for anyone to see.
- **Gangs and bribes.** Gangs lived on cash and on selling illegal things. Bribes need money, and someone with lasting power to pay it to. None of that exists here.
- **Cutting corners.** Nobody earns anything from cheap materials, unsafe work, or things that break.
- **Showing off.** Being rich doesn't impress anyone, so there is no point in grabbing more.

Some things were never about money: jealousy, rage, cruelty, drinking, wanting to control another person. Those are still here. But the things that made them worse are gone: fear, debt, being worn out, having nowhere else to go. People still hurt each other sometimes. It happens less often, and nobody does it to make a living.

**Three rules.**
Don't harm. Don't lie to people. Don't get in other people's way. Everything else is up to you: helping, finishing what you start, even joining in at all.

**Why doesn't it all fall apart?**
Because most people don't like doing nothing. When people feel safe and have free time, most of them start fixing, growing, teaching or building something. Studies show that people do their best work when they chose it themselves, when they are getting better at it, and when it matters to someone. Pressure and pay can even make people care less. Volunteers have already built a huge free encyclopedia, a map of the whole world, and much of the software the internet runs on. And not everyone has to join in. Enough people do.

**Who provides the food, homes and care?**
The same kinds of people as before: farmers who like farming, nurses who like nursing, builders who like building. Machines do the heavy, boring and dangerous parts. Three things make it enough. Knowledge is free, so anyone can learn these skills. Things last a long time, so far fewer need to be made. And no effort goes into selling. The old world already grew enough food for everyone. It just didn't reach the people who had no money.

**Where do the materials come from, and who gets them?**
Mostly from things that already exist. The old world dug up huge amounts of metal, used it for a short time, and threw it away. Here, old things are taken apart and the materials are used again. Every object comes with a note saying what it is made of and how to take it apart. Old rubbish dumps are the richest mines.

Nobody owns materials. Each town has a store of them, like a library. You take what your project needs, and the material goes back when the thing is no longer used. The Earth-monitor (explained below) shows what each store has, so when one town runs short, another sends some over.

Sometimes there really isn't enough of something. Then it goes where it is needed most. Medicine comes before gadgets. If a design needs a rare material, people try to change the design.

Some new mining still happens, because a little material is lost every time it is reused. But a mine is very hard to undo. So it is decided slowly, by many towns together, and the people living nearby can say no. Machines go underground first.

**About three hours a month.**
That is roughly how much work each person would need to do, if everyone shared the jobs nobody wants: drains, night shifts, cleaning. Machines do most of it. The number is small because so much old work was only there because of money: billing, selling, adverts, guarding, managing, and making things that were meant to break. The work people choose, like caring, crafting, teaching and research, takes many more hours. Nobody counts those.

**What if nobody shows up?**
Every job has a long list of people who have said "I might do this." If the first person can't, the next one is asked. If nobody has signed up, the job moves higher on everyone's page until someone takes it. Real danger is treated as an emergency, and many people come quickly. Jobs are split into small pieces, so nobody is stuck with a nasty one for life.

**A shared page instead of bosses.**
There is one open record called the Earth-monitor. It shows what is broken and what is needed, from a blocked drain to a whole river. The information comes from sensors and from reports that anyone can add or question. It never tells anyone what to do. There are no points and no rewards. It counts people but does not know who they are, and your personal page stays on your own device.

A typical morning goes like this. If you already have something going, like a project, a visit or a plan, you carry on with that. If you don't, you open your page and look at what you could help with today. It shows a few small tasks that fit your skills and are close to where you live. You pick one, or none. The list is short on purpose, and it has an end.

**Computers and the internet.**
You get a computer or a phone the same way you get anything else: from your town's store. There is no new model every year. A device is built to last for decades. You can open it, repair it, and swap one part without replacing the rest. The designs are public, so any workshop can make or fix one. When you no longer need it, it goes back, and the next person uses it.

The internet still exists, but it works very differently from the one in 2026:

- **No adverts.** Nothing is sold, so nobody pays to get your attention.
- **Nobody follows you around.** Your information stays on your own device. No company collects it, because there are no companies.
- **Nobody owns it.** The network is looked after by towns, the way roads are. The information is stored in small pieces on everyone's devices, so there is no single place that can be switched off or taken over.
- **It lets you go.** Pages have an end. Nothing is built to keep you scrolling, and nothing gets pushed to the top because it makes people angry.
- **Everything can be copied.** Every book, film, song and lesson is free to read, watch, change and share.
- **It says how things were made.** You can see whether a person or a machine made something, and where it came from.
- **Almost no spam or scams.** Most of those were about getting your money.

You can also switch it all off, for a day or for years. Some people do.

One thing is slower. With nobody racing to sell the next gadget, new devices appear less often. Most people have not noticed.

**Who decides things?**
For small things, the person doing the work decides the details. Others can object, but only for a real reason: it hurts someone, it misleads someone, or it gets in someone's way. Bigger questions go to a small group of ordinary people picked at random, like a jury. They study the question, explain their decision in public, and then the group ends. Experts give advice but don't decide. The more people a decision affects, and the harder it is to undo, the more slowly it is made. Ireland has used randomly chosen citizens' groups like this for some of its hardest questions.

**What stops bossy people?**
To boss people around, you need someone who can't say no. Here everyone can say no, because nobody's food or home depends on obeying. No position lasts forever. The records are open. Anyone can leave, or start their own version next door. There are leaders, but only for as long as people choose to follow them.

**Borrowed, not owned.**
An ordinary home is yours for as long as you live in it. When you leave, someone else moves in. Everything is built so it can be repaired and taken apart.

**What about the homes everyone wants?**
Some homes are wanted by many people, like a cabin by a lake or a flat with a sea view. Those work more like a booking. You stay for an agreed time, and then the next person arrives. That way everyone who wants a turn gets one.

But the queues are shorter than you might expect. In the old world a sea view was worth a fortune, and most of the reasons had little to do with the sea:

- **It showed success.** A view told everyone you had made it. Here a home says nothing about you.
- **It was an investment.** People bought views in order to sell them later for more. Nothing is sold here.
- **It was kept rare.** Many of the best places stood empty most of the year, as second homes and holiday lets. Now they are lived in all year, so far more people get a turn.
- **You only chose once.** Buying a home was the biggest choice of a lifetime, so people wanted the best one. Here you can move again next year.
- **You get used to it.** After a few months, most people stop looking out of the window. Many find they would sooner live near friends, a bakery or a forest.

The sea is still lovely, and people still go. But "the best home" has turned into "the home that suits me right now." That is a different answer for everyone, and it changes.

**Culture is one long remix.**
Anything that is shared can be copied and changed. The original stays as it was. Giving credit works like a map: it shows where things came from. Nobody needs to sell a song to eat, and that ended most of the fight about art made by machines. Machines help people make things. And people now make the things machines can't: songs sung only once, works that take many years, things made for one person.

**When someone does harm.**
First, whoever is closest stops it. Then people look for what caused it and change something. Plane crash investigators work the same way: they look for causes, not for someone to blame. Nobody is punished just to make them suffer. But there are limits. Someone who keeps misusing a thing can lose the right to use it. And nobody has to trust or forgive them.

**How did it happen?**
Slowly, over many generations. There was no revolution. Nobody banned money. The basic needs became free one at a time, and the easy ones went first.

- **Knowledge.** This was first, because copying costs nothing. Free encyclopedias, free software and free lessons already existed in the old world. Later, research, medicine recipes and machine designs were shared too.
- **Energy.** Sunlight and wind send no bill. Once the panels and turbines were built, and built to last, power cost almost nothing to make. Towns began to own their power together.
- **Things.** People repaired more and bought less. Tool libraries and shared cars spread. Goods were made to last, so each year fewer new ones were needed.
- **Food.** The world already grew enough. Shared gardens, shared kitchens and passing on what was left over covered more and more of it. Machines took over much of the farm work.
- **Getting around.** Some cities made buses and trams free. Others followed, because it was cheaper than selling and checking tickets.
- **Health.** Many countries already treated people without a bill. Open medicine recipes, more people trained, and stopping illness early made care cheaper every decade.
- **Homes.** This was the last and the hardest, because most people's savings were in their houses. Empty buildings were put to use. Towns and housing groups built homes that were never sold. As more people had a secure home, rents fell.

Each step made the next one easier. Every need you could meet without money was one less reason to take a bad job. That gave people more free time, and they used some of it to build more shared things.

The order mattered. The safety net had to come before the old economy shrank. Where jobs and savings disappeared first, people were frightened, and they held on to money more tightly. Some towns moved too fast and had to go back.

The people who earned from things being scarce fought it for a long time. They were not robbed. What they owned slowly lost its value, the way a shop loses value when nobody needs to buy there.

And over the years, enough people stopped wanting what they were told to want.

**What it doesn't fix.**
Grief, shyness, loneliness, and not knowing what to do with your life. People here are not happy all the time. They got used to this world, the way people get used to anything. Some small difficulties are left in on purpose, because a world with no difficulties at all turned out to feel empty.

**Where it has problems.**
The same few generous people still do too much. When many people volunteer, each one may think someone else has already gone. In a small town, a lot of counting can start to feel like being watched. Three hours is only an estimate. And for serious violence, there is still no better answer than neighbours stepping in.

The rest of this blog is small stories from one town. Each one tests one of these points. It's a thought exercise. Don't take it too seriously.
