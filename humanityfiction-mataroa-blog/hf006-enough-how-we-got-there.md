# HF006: Enough. How we got there

> Published on Oct 25, 2026

I cut my hand sanding the bench slats. Not badly, but enough to need stitches.

The health room is two streets away, next to the library. No queue. No forms. Aino was there, a retired nurse who stitches people as a hobby, the way some people do crosswords. A small machine checked the cut first and said it was clean. Aino did the rest.

While she worked, I asked her when this became normal. She's old enough to remember when it wasn't.

"In my first job, there was never enough of anything," she said. "Not enough beds, not enough nurses, not enough time. People waited for months. Some didn't come at all, because they were afraid of the bill."

So how did it change?

"Slowly. And then all at once."

Here's the short version of what she told me.

## Not all scarcity was real

Some of it was. Bodies are complicated, and skilled hands take years to train. But a lot of the scarcity was made: by rules, by money, and by habit.

Healthcare was one of the clearest examples. The same pattern showed up almost everywhere.

## But everyone still had to earn a living

That was my next question. In the old world, a nurse needed her salary. A hospital needed its budget. A drug company needed its profits. Nobody could just stop.

"Nobody did stop," Aino said. "The new way grew inside the old one. Like a hobby."

Roughly, it went like this:

- **Sharing was often cheaper, even in the old system.** Companies already built free, open-source software together, because building it alone cost more. Health systems slowly worked out that paying once for research and sharing the results was cheaper than paying high prices forever.
- **Things got cheap to make.** Machines, solar power and digital copying made many things almost free to produce. When something costs almost nothing to make, it gets hard to sell. Printed encyclopedias learned this early.
- **People did it on the side.** Aino worked her paid shifts and spent Saturdays at a free clinic. Retired people, students and part-timers had time, and they used it. Every shared thing meant someone, somewhere, needed a little less money.
- **Survival got separated from jobs, bit by bit.** Some places tried giving people a basic income with no strings attached. Finland, among others, ran a small experiment like that in 2017–2018. Shared housing, free transport and free care did the same thing in other ways. Every basic need covered was one less reason to take a bad job.
- **Crises helped.** In every disaster, people shared freely for a while. Each time, a bit of it stayed.

Then it started feeding itself. The less money people needed, the less they had to work for it. The less they had to work, the more time they had for shared things. And the more shared things there were, the less money anyone needed.

Not everyone liked it. People who earned from things being scarce fought it for a long time. For decades the two systems ran side by side, and most people lived in both.

"Nobody switched," Aino said. "One day you just noticed you hadn't paid for anything in a while."

## Knowledge got unlocked

A medicine is mostly a recipe. Once someone has worked out how to make it, copying the recipe costs nothing. But recipes were locked behind patents, so a pill that cost little to make could cost a fortune to buy.

There were early hints that it didn't have to be this way. The people who discovered insulin sold the patent for one dollar each, because they wanted it to belong to everyone. When the polio vaccine came out, its creator was asked who owned the patent. His answer, more or less: the people. You can't patent the sun.

Eventually, that became the normal way. Every medicine, every device design, every treatment guide is open, free to copy, improve and share again. Like open-source software, but for bodies.

## Machines took the routine work

A lot of a doctor's time used to go to things a machine can do: reading scans, checking results, spotting patterns. And a lot of everyone's time went to paperwork about who pays for what.

Machines took the first. The paperwork simply disappeared once nobody was billing anybody.

That freed people for the part machines are bad at: holding someone's hand at 3 a.m.

Equipment changed too. Old hospitals sometimes couldn't even get the manuals to repair their own machines. Now everything is built to be opened, fixed, and fixed again.

## Learning stopped being a gate

In the old world, the number of people who could train was limited. Places were capped, training was long and expensive, and many who wanted to help never got through the door.

When learning became free, lots of people learned a little, and some learned a lot. Now every street has a few people who can stitch a wound, and every town has people who chose medicine simply because they love it.

Nobody is on duty because they have to be. But so many people know how that someone always wants to.

## Fixing things before they broke

The cheapest illness is the one that never happens.

Much of the old system got paid when people got sick, not when they stayed well. Once nobody earned anything from sickness, keeping people well became the obvious thing to do. The Earth-monitor watches water, air and food, and a lot of illness now stops before it starts.

## The same pattern everywhere

- **Food:** the world already grew enough food for everyone. It just didn't reach the people who were hungry. Measure the need, share the surplus. (See: courgettes.)
- **Housing:** empty homes and homeless people existed side by side in the same cities. Shared, durable homes ended that.
- **Energy:** sunlight keeps arriving, and nobody owns it.
- **Education:** every book and every lesson is shared. Teaching is a hobby too.

Each time, the trick was the same: unlock the knowledge, let machines do the boring part, let anyone learn, fix things early, and stop earning from things being scarce.

## What's still scarce

Time. Skilled hands for difficult surgery. Someone who'll sit with you when you're scared.

Abundance doesn't mean infinite. It means enough, for everyone, most of the time, and knowing quickly when there isn't, so others can come and help.

* * *

When she was done, I asked what I owed her.

She gave me the look people gave the courgette man.

"Tell me if it goes red," she said.
