# HF019: Everything affects everything

> Published on Nov 9, 2026

The canal finally happened. The one somebody proposed "for the joy of it," back when the Earth-monitor was empty.

On its first rainy night, it overflowed into the meadow behind the library. The one with the ground-nesting birds.

In the old world, the next morning would have been about one question: *whose fault was this?* Someone would have been found, blamed, maybe punished. Everyone else would have felt relieved it wasn't them.

Here, the question was different: *what caused this?*

## Faults and causes

By lunchtime, the answer was on the Earth-monitor, and it wasn't one person.

The canal crew had used an old map that didn't show the meadow's slope. The meadow's "no benches, nesting birds" note didn't appear on the canal crew's pages, because the two projects weren't linked. The rain was heavier than any forecast. And the soil was still soft from autumn.

Jonne, who'd dug most of the canal, felt terrible. Aino told him: "Guilt won't drain a meadow. Help me find the causes instead."

So they did. The map got updated. Nearby projects now show up on each other's pages. A small gate went into the canal. By spring, the meadow was dry again.

Faults, you punish. Causes, you fix.

## Everything affects everything

Behind this is a very old idea called determinism. In its simplest form: everything that happens is caused by things that happened before.

Your genes, your childhood, your language, the street you grew up on, what you ate this morning, whether you slept well, what the person before you said. All of it shapes what you think and do next. And what you do next shapes everything else, including your own future self. Everything affects everything, and itself, all the time.

The philosopher Schopenhauer put it neatly: we can do what we want, but we can't choose what we want.

## Is it true?

Nobody really knows. Physicists argue about it. Philosophers argue even more. Maybe there's some randomness at the bottom of things. Maybe there's a little free will hiding in there somewhere.

Here, nobody minds much. The philosopher William James suggested that a good way to judge an idea is to ask what difference it makes when you live by it. And living as if everything has causes turns out to work remarkably well.

Even if it isn't completely true, it's true enough to be useful. And useful enough to change a world.

## What changes when you see it

**Blame turns into curiosity.** Instead of "who's guilty?", people ask "what led to this?" Causes are much more interesting than culprits, and much easier to fix.

**Pride and guilt get quieter.** Your first gifts, your worst habits, your luck: you didn't choose most of the things that made you. That's why neither pride nor guilt is very helpful. Nobody earned their starting point.

**"Deserve" stops making sense.** Eevi's question from the old world museum fits here: "Why would you have to deserve food?" If nobody chose their beginning, nobody deserves a worse one.

**Difficult people make more sense.** Pekka wasn't born grumpy. Thirty years of being hated as a parking officer will do that to anyone. Marja understands this, which is why she keeps going.

**You get kinder to yourself.** Your bad days have causes too: sleep, hunger, old hurts. Seeing them is the first step to changing them, and it's much gentler than self-blame.

**The design becomes the point.** If people are shaped by their surroundings, then the surroundings are where change happens. That's the whole idea behind this world: the problem was never the people, it was the design.

The old-world philosopher Spinoza thought something similar centuries ago: once you understand the causes of a feeling, it loses some of its hold on you.

## Doesn't this let people off the hook?

Not really. Harm still gets stopped. A determinist still locks the door, still pulls a child back from the road, still steps in when someone is hurting others.

What changes is the *why*. You don't stop harm to make someone suffer for being bad. You stop it because it's harmful, and then you look for what caused it, so it happens less.

And responsibility doesn't disappear. It just faces forward. Not "who's to blame for the past?" but "who can help with what comes next?" Response-ability, as some people like to spell it.

## Even if it isn't true

Here's the part that makes it inspiring rather than gloomy.

If everything affects everything, then you are a cause too. Every small thing you do ripples outward: a sanded bench slat, a note left for the next person, a cheerful "huh" instead of a sulk, an hour on a bench with someone grumpy.

You didn't write your own beginning. Nobody does. But you are one of the causes of everything that comes after you.

That's enough to drive change, whether or not it's the whole truth.

* * *

Eevi came to look at the meadow after it dried out.

"So the flood was nobody's fault?" she asked.

Aino thought about it. "Not a fault," she said. "Lots of causes. And now we're some of them too."
