# HF017: The obvious list

> Published on Nov 9, 2026

Eevi's learning group had a question for the week: *What does a person actually need?*

They spent an afternoon on it, argued a lot, and pinned their answer on the library wall:

- Food, water, sleep, warmth
- A place with a door you can close
- Some people who love you, and someone who'd notice if you went missing
- Being useful to someone
- Deciding your own days
- Something to get better at
- Play, and things that are beautiful
- Trees, quiet, and safe streets
- Time that isn't in a hurry
- Knowing tomorrow is okay

"Is that it?" Eevi asked. "It's quite short."

It is. And then I told her the funny part: almost the same list is pinned up in libraries all over the world. It's the list everything here was built around.

She looked at it for a long time. "So why didn't people just do that before?"

## Everyone knew

That's the laughable part. The old world knew the list. Grandmothers knew it. Every self-help book knew it. Scientists measured it: one study followed people for over eighty years and found that good relationships mattered more for a long, happy life than almost anything else.

People knew it. They just didn't build for it.

They built for other things. Cities were designed around cars. Days were designed around offices. Whole economies were designed around selling more stuff, every year, forever.

It was like building a restaurant around the needs of the cash register.

## Measuring the wrong thing

The old world's favourite number was GDP: how much money changed hands. A car crash raised it, because of the repairs. A quiet afternoon with friends didn't count at all.

Some people noticed. In 1968, the American politician Robert Kennedy said GDP measured everything except what makes life worth living. In the 1970s, the economist Richard Easterlin noticed that as countries got richer, people didn't get happier nearly as fast. Bhutan started talking about "Gross National Happiness." In 2019, New Zealand made a "wellbeing budget". And Finland, a cold, dark country with a lot of sauna, kept topping the world happiness rankings.

They were small sparks. But each one showed that the list could be measured, and built for.

## Building around the list

It happened slowly, town by town, the way everything here happens. During the Great Shrug, more and more people noticed that buying more didn't make them happier. Towns started asking a simple question about each item on the list: *Do we make this easy, or hard?*

- **Sleep:** quiet nights, dark streets, and nobody racing to an office at seven.
- **Movement:** streets for walking, so moving is just how you get somewhere.
- **People:** benches, small neighbourhoods, and shared jobs like drain crews, where friendships happen by accident.
- **Being useful:** three small tasks every morning, for anyone who wants them.
- **Choice:** nothing expected of anyone.
- **Trees:** one old-world rule of thumb said every home should see three trees from the window and be within 300 metres of a park. Here that became a minimum.
- **Beauty:** art and music everywhere, free.
- **Knowing tomorrow is okay:** the floor. Nobody falls through it.

And the Earth-monitor started measuring the list instead of money. Not *how much did we sell today?* but *how many people saw a tree today, slept well, or had someone to talk to?* Always counted, never watched.

## Laughably obvious

Every time a town made the list easier, people got healthier, kinder and calmer, and needed less stuff. Other towns copied it. It spread the way good ideas spread here: by working.

Looking back, it's almost embarrassing. For centuries, humanity was building elaborate machines for making people want things, when it could have just been building around what people needed.

As Eevi put it: "So you had the answer, and you just… didn't?"

Before she left, Eevi added one line to the bottom of her group's list, in green pencil:

*Interesting leaves.*
