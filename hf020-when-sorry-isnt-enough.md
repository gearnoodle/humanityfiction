# HF020: When sorry isn't enough

> Published on Nov 10, 2026

Last week, Eevi stopped coming to the library on Tuesdays.

I noticed because Tuesday is when she usually sits with Aino, drawing things in her notebook and asking questions nobody else thinks to ask.

When I asked Aino, she said there had been an argument.

Jonne had borrowed Eevi's notebook without asking. He'd shown some of her drawings to his friends. They'd laughed at one of them.

He apologised. Properly, apparently. He looked genuinely sorry.

Eevi didn't want to see him.

"So what happens now?" I asked.

Aino shrugged. "Nothing, until something happens."

I wasn't sure what she meant.

## No punishment?

In the old world, we had a fairly standard collection of responses to people who hurt other people.

Punishment. Fines. Suspensions. Expulsion. Prisons.

Sometimes these protected people. Sometimes they made things worse. Often they did a bit of both.

The underlying idea was that someone had done something wrong, and therefore something unpleasant should happen to them.

Here, we start somewhere else.

First, stop the harm.

Then understand what happened.

Then work out what needs to change.

There is no requirement to forgive anyone. There is no requirement to become friends again. And there is certainly no requirement to pretend everything is fine.

## Understanding isn't excusing

This connects to something we've discussed before: everything has causes.

Jonne hadn't suddenly become cruel. He'd wanted to impress his friends. He'd thought the drawings were funny. He hadn't considered what it would feel like for Eevi.

Understanding that doesn't make what he did okay.

Aino put it rather neatly:

"Knowing why someone broke your window doesn't mean you have to let them into your house."

The causes help us understand what happened. They don't remove the need to protect people.

## The right to say no

This is perhaps the most important difference.

Eevi doesn't owe Jonne another chance.

She doesn't have to accept his apology, listen to his explanation, or help him become a better person.

She can simply say no.

Jonne can learn from what happened. He can change how he behaves. He can apologise again, if that's appropriate.

But he cannot demand a particular response.

A relationship requires two people. An apology only requires one.

## What happens when it keeps happening?

Of course, one incident is relatively easy.

The harder question is what happens when somebody repeatedly hurts others.

Imagine someone who keeps borrowing things without asking, ignores boundaries, and makes other people uncomfortable.

We don't need to decide whether they're a bad person.

We need to make sure they can't keep doing it.

Perhaps they lose access to a particular shared space for a while. Perhaps someone accompanies them when they use a tool that has been misused. Perhaps a group decides it no longer wants to work with them.

These aren't punishments designed to make someone suffer.

They're boundaries designed to prevent further harm.

And they can be reviewed.

Nobody gets a permanent label. Nobody is beyond the possibility of change. But nobody has to volunteer to be hurt while waiting for that change.

## The difference between forgiveness and trust

There's a curious thing about the old world's approach to apologies.

People were often expected to accept them, move on, and restore the relationship.

But trust doesn't work like that.

You can forgive someone and still not trust them with your bicycle.

You can understand someone and still not want to spend an afternoon with them.

You can wish someone well and prefer to live three streets away.

These aren't contradictions.

They're just different things.

Trust is built from experience. It can't be demanded, purchased, or awarded because somebody has said the right words.

## A society without enemies

Perhaps this is one of the hardest things to learn when building a different society.

We don't need to divide people into good and bad.

We need to distinguish between behaviour that is safe and behaviour that isn't.

We don't need revenge. We need protection, understanding and, where possible, repair.

And sometimes repair means bringing people back together.

Sometimes it means giving someone the tools to behave differently.

Sometimes it means leaving two people alone, in separate parts of town, perfectly happy never to meet again.

All three are acceptable outcomes.

## Back at the library

I saw Eevi on Thursday.

She was drawing a small bird on the pink bench. Jonne was sitting on the other side, repairing a camera.

They weren't talking.

"Are you friends again?" I asked.

"No," she said.

Jonne looked up.

"That's okay," he said. "I think I understand."

Eevi went back to her drawing.

A few minutes later, she showed Aino the bird.

It had a very large beak.

"Interesting," Aino said.

"It's a courgette bird," Eevi explained.

Nobody asked Jonne to leave.

Nobody asked Eevi to forgive him.

And nobody had to pretend that Thursday was the same as Tuesday.

Perhaps that's enough.
