# HF035: Where do the big things go?

> Published on Nov 11, 2026

On Monday a new proposal appeared on the Earth-monitor.

> **Spaceship centre** · Location: meadow behind the library · Proposed by: someone with a wallet full of leaves

Within a minute the map had answered. Ground-nesting birds, April to July. Too far north: rockets like to start near the equator, where the Earth's spin gives them a free push. Six hundred people inside the noise circle. And the nearest launch site, far to the south, is half empty.

Nobody had to tell Eevi no. The map did it, politely, before any person got involved.

"It's still a good idea," she said.

It got me thinking about how the really big things get placed. I've sat on the railway panel for a while now, so I've seen some of it from inside.

## First: do we need one?

Before "where" comes "whether".

The old world had five car factories making nearly the same car, because five companies each needed their own. A lot of big buildings existed for reasons like that.

So the first thing anyone checks is the need. How many are there already, how full are they, and could an existing one do the job? Most proposals for big things end right here, quietly, with a number.

## The map answers first

Everything about the land is measured. Water, ground, wind, sun, noise, what lives there, what's already built, which materials are on which shelves, how many people are near. (How many. Never who. And there are grey patches where someone has switched it all off. The map plans around them.)

So a proposal doesn't start as an argument. You put a pin on the map, and the map shows what the pin touches: whose water, whose quiet, which birds. Move the pin and it all changes. Children do this for fun.

It means the obvious mistakes die early and in private. Nobody has to be the person who says no.

## The people who know

Maps don't have ideas, though. Somebody has to say: we should build one of these, about here, about this size.

Anyone can. But some people have made a study of it. They've spent years learning how reactors behave, or rivers, or what a school does to a neighbourhood. When they suggest something, others listen.

Why listen? Because you can check. Every suggestion a person has made stays on the record, next to what actually happened. Raili has made thirty-three predictions about things underground. Thirty-one were right. The two she got wrong are listed first, at her own request.

That's all a reputation is here: a visible history of being right, and of being wrong honestly. It works in one subject only. Raili on drains carries weight. Raili on schools is just an opinion. It can't be turned into anything else, and it fades if you stop.

They suggest. They don't decide.

## Then the two questions

How many people does it touch, and how hard is it to undo?

- **A school:** touches a neighbourhood, and is easy to change. The neighbourhood sorts it out. If two groups disagree, there can be two schools.
- **A workshop for building trains:** touches a region. A lottery panel, a few weeks.
- **A data centre:** touches a town, mostly through its heat, so that gets planned first.
- **A reactor:** touches many towns for a very long time. The slowest process we have.
- **A spaceship centre:** there is only one sky.

## Where it wants to be

Big things have preferences of their own. The rocket wants the equator. The reactor wants still ground and water. The factory wants to be near the materials it eats. The data centre wants cold, and neighbours who can use its warmth. Helsinki was heating homes with a data centre's waste heat by 2010.

A lot of placing is just listening to that. The old world often put things where the land was cheap or the tax was low. Neither exists here, so what's left is physics and neighbours.

## Who lives next to it

In the old world, the answer to "where does the unpleasant thing go?" was the same as the drains: next to whoever had the least power to refuse.

Here everyone can refuse, which sounds as if nothing unpleasant could ever be built. It turns out not to be true.

The old world had one good example. When Finland looked for a place to bury its spent nuclear fuel for a hundred thousand years, the law let the local council say no. In 2000 the council of Eurajoki voted yes, twenty to seven. They had lived beside the power plant for twenty years and knew what they were agreeing to.

So places aren't chosen. They offer. A town looks at the map and says: we'd host that, on these conditions. Often there are several offers, and for odd reasons. One town wanted a reactor because its retired engineers were bored.

And the old rule holds: the people nearest aren't outvoted. If it's bad for them, it gets made right or it goes somewhere else.

## How it ends

Every proposal for a big thing includes its own taking-apart: how it comes down, where the materials go, what the ground will be afterwards. If nobody can say how to unbuild it, it isn't ready to be built.

## What still goes wrong

It's slow. The railway panel settled the route in three weeks and the station benches in eleven.

Maps can be wrong. The canal flooded the meadow because of an old one.

And people with fine reputations can all be wrong together. So big things get built in steps, smallest first, with a way back at each one.

* * *

Eevi revised her proposal.

> **Spaceship centre (small)** · Location: roof of the library shed · Launches: paper only

Nobody had a serious objection. Raili noted that the roof would hold.

The first launch was on Thursday. The rocket came down in the meadow.

The birds have filed no objection.
