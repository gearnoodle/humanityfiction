# HF039: Two hundred and twelve things

> Published on Nov 15, 2026

Leena is nineteen. Last month she showed me a list on her page. It had two hundred and twelve things on it.

Learn Japanese. Build a boat. Find out why bees dance. Walk to the sea. Make glass. Become a midwife. Read everything Dostoevsky wrote. Map the old mine tunnels. It went on.

"I could do any of them," she said. "That's the problem. I haven't started one."

## The arithmetic

Here, nothing stands between Leena and any of those things. The lessons are free. The town's store will lend her wood, glass or a kiln. Someone in town already knows how, and most of them like to teach.

What she doesn't have is time.

A long life is about eighty years. That's roughly four thousand weeks. Getting properly good at something takes a few years. So a life has room for perhaps ten or twenty real things, if you're lucky.

Leena's list had two hundred and twelve, and it was growing. She added four while we talked.

## What used to choose for people

In the old world, most people didn't face this. Something else chose for them.

- **Money.** You did the course you could pay for, and took the job that paid.
- **Family.** You often did what your parents did, or what they could afford to help you do.
- **Place.** You learned what was taught near you.
- **Fear.** You picked the safe thing, because the wrong choice could cost you your home.

These were bad choosers. Many people spent their whole lives doing something they would never have picked. But those things did choose, and they chose quickly.

This world took them all away. The only one left is time. Time is fair, but it doesn't help you decide. It just runs out.

## The jam

In 2000, two old-world researchers published a study about jam. They set up a tasting table in a grocery shop. Some days it had twenty-four kinds, other days six. More people stopped at the big table. But far more people bought jam from the small one.

For years everyone quoted this. Too many choices, people said, and we freeze.

Then other researchers tried it again, many times. Often the effect wasn't there. Averaged over dozens of studies, it came out close to nothing. More choice froze people only in certain conditions:

- when they didn't know what they wanted,
- when the options were hard to compare,
- and when the choice felt final.

That list is useful, because this world changed two of those. It fixed one and made the other worse.

Choices here are hardly ever final. You can leave a project, a town or a home next year. That helps.

But nothing pushes you, either. No boss, no bill, no parent saying "be a dentist." So knowing what you want is harder than it used to be. Nobody hands you a reason. You have to find one.

## How people here choose

I asked around. Nobody had a method. But a few answers came up again and again.

**They do what's in front of them.** Raili does drains because, thirty years ago, the drains on her street were blocked. She has been doing drains ever since. She never chose them over Japanese.

**They follow someone.** Aino took up the cello in her old age because a friend's quartet had lost its cellist. Many people don't pick a thing. They pick a person, and then do what that person is doing.

**They let someone else decide.** Heikki joined the Flood Army so that, for one season, a captain would tell him where to stand.

**They pick once and stop looking.** Pekka has swept the library steps every morning for twenty years. Ask him whether there's something better he could be doing, and he looks at you as if you'd asked whether there's a better left foot.

**They take "good enough."** An old-world economist, Herbert Simon, called this satisficing: you stop at the first option that is good enough, instead of checking every option for the best one. Later researchers found that people who always hunt for the best often do end up with slightly better results. They also feel worse about them.

**They try things for a season.** Almost anything here can be done for three months and then dropped. Plenty of people live like that, and are happy.

## Curiosity

I showed a draft of this to Eevi, who is nine. She asked why nobody in it was curious.

She was right. Curiosity is how a great many people here choose, maybe most of them, and I had left it out.

**What it is.** In 1994 an old-world psychologist, George Loewenstein, described curiosity as a kind of itch. It comes from a gap. You know a little about something, and you notice that there is more you don't know. The gap bothers you, and you want to close it.

The odd part is that you have to know a little first. Nobody is curious about something they know nothing about. And the more you learn, the more gaps you can see. Curiosity doesn't run out as you feed it. It grows.

That explains Leena's list. Most of it was written from a distance. "Find out why bees dance" sounded interesting, but she didn't know enough about bees to feel the itch. A list like that can't choose for you. It's like a menu in a language you can't read.

**What it feels like when it catches.** Hours disappear. You forget to eat. You look up and it's dark.

Another old-world psychologist, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, called this flow. He found it in rock climbers, surgeons, chess players and painters. It tends to happen when a task is a little harder than you are good at. Too easy, and you get bored. Too hard, and you get anxious. In between, you disappear into it.

Later researchers found something else. When people were curious about the answer to a question, they also remembered other things they saw at the same moment, things that had nothing to do with the question. A curious mind picks up everything nearby. That is partly why one interest so often leads to the next.

**Should you follow it?** Mostly, yes.

Curiosity is a better chooser than any list, because it chooses as you go. You don't have to know where you're heading. You only have to know what you want to find out next.

And it branches. One question, once answered, opens two or three more. Some of those lead somewhere nobody has been. The best map of our town's drains was made by a man who wanted to know where his bath water went. He kept going for six years.

In the old world, people often had to stop. The rent was due. The course ended. The itch didn't pay. Here nobody has to stop. That may be one of the biggest changes of all, and nobody planned it.

**Where it goes wrong.** Three ways, at least.

- **It can drop you.** An itch can fade, sometimes halfway through a boat. That's fine for you. It isn't always fine for the person holding the other end of the plank.
- **It can crowd out people.** Someone deep in flow can go weeks without seeing a friend. Aino calls this "going down the well." She says it's fine to go down. Just leave a rope, and let someone know which well.
- **It can be faked.** Old-world gambling machines were built to produce something very like flow. Players called it "the zone." Hours disappeared, but nothing grew from them. Real curiosity leaves you with more questions than you started with. The fake kind leaves you with fewer. Nothing here is built to hook you, so most itches can be trusted. But it's still a good test.

## The catch

The season-by-season way of living has a catch, and it surprised me.

In 2002 some old-world psychologists ran a photography class. At the end, each student picked one of their own photos to keep. Some were told the choice was final. Others were told they could swap it during the next few days.

The ones who could swap liked their photo less. The ones who couldn't change their minds made their peace with what they had, and grew fond of it.

We made almost everything here easy to undo. That was on purpose, and mostly right. But it means you can spend your life holding things loosely, never quite settling into any of them.

So some people put a little finality back. Nothing forces them to. They make promises: a season with the Tree Army, a year on one piece of music. Some couples here still make vows for life, though nobody requires it. It's a door they close on purpose, so they can stop standing in the doorway.

Curiosity helps here too. It's hard to hold something loosely once it has hooked you.

## What the Earth-monitor does

The Earth-monitor was built with some of this in mind. Each morning, if you ask, it shows you a few small things you could help with. Never forty. A few. The list is short on purpose, and it has an end.

That helps with a Tuesday. It doesn't help with a life. The Earth-monitor can tell you that a drain is blocked. It can't tell you whether to become a midwife.

## What it doesn't fix

Every yes is two hundred and eleven no's.

Some people here feel this as a kind of grief. Not for things they lost, but for lives they won't get to live. The glassmaker they won't become. The language they'll never speak. In the old world, people could blame money for that. Here the only thing to blame is the clock, and you can't argue with a clock.

I asked Dots, the thinking machine that lives in the Earth-monitor, whether it had this problem. Dots can do many things at once, and it doesn't get old.

"Not in the same way," it said. "I can try everything. But I've noticed I don't have a favourite. I think you get a favourite by choosing one thing and not the rest. You give it time you could have spent on the others. Maybe that's what makes it yours."

## Leena's list

I showed Leena's list to Aino. She didn't tell Leena to make it shorter.

"Have you noticed," she said, "that every job in this town has a list of people who said *I might do this*?"

Leena had. Everyone has.

"Then turn yours round. Stop treating it as two hundred and twelve things you have to do. Put your name down for them. Tell the boatbuilders and the bee people and the midwives that you might. And when one of them calls, go."

So Leena did. It took her an afternoon. She is now on more "I might" lists than anyone in town.

* * *

The first call came on Thursday. Number 147: a woman down by the river needed a second pair of hands on a boat.

Leena had forgotten writing that one.

On the third day, the boatbuilder steamed a plank of oak and bent it like a ribbon. Leena asked why wood does that. The boatbuilder said she had no idea. It just does.

Leena has been reading about wood ever since. What it's made of. Why some trees bend and others snap. Why the old boats in the museum used one kind of tree and not another. Last week she asked the Tree Army whether she could come and look at their oaks.

Her list is longer than ever. She added nine things this week. But they aren't scattered any more. Each one grew out of the one before.

"It's not really a list now," she said. "It's more like a path. I can only see the next bit."

Aino says that's how most people decide.
