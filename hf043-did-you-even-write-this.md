# HF043: Did you even write this?

> Published on Nov 22, 2026

This letter is made up. Like the three wise men, it's made of real parts. [WM000] Every line in it is something I've heard from someone, or said to myself at six in the morning.

It arrived, in my head, on a Sunday.

> Dear MJ,
>
> I've read your blog for three weeks. I liked it. I sent it to two friends. Then I noticed.
>
> Every post has the same rhythm. Short sentence. Short sentence. A list. A study from "the old world", with a year. A grumpy man with a parking ticket. A wise old nurse. A nine-year-old who says something perfect at the end.
>
> You didn't write this. A machine did.
>
> One of your three rules is "don't deceive". Everything in your town "says how it was made". So where does it say how *this* was made? You wrote a whole post about the children's book you've wanted to write for seventeen years. [HF033] Then you got a machine to write it for you.
>
> You even wrote a post about why people were angry with machines, and decided they were mostly wrong. [HF038] Of course you did. The machine helped you decide.
>
> That machine learned to write from millions of people who were never asked and never paid. Novelists. Teachers. Someone who once wrote a careful post to help a stranger fix a boiler. In your town, you've fixed that. In this world, you haven't, and you're using it anyway.
>
> Here's what really gets me. You spent a few minutes asking for each post. You're asking me to spend fifteen reading it. Why should I spend my time on what you didn't spend your time writing?
>
> You write about once-only songs, bad singing at birthdays, and things with a body in them. And you couldn't be bothered to write your own sentences.
>
> I didn't want a machine's idea of a better humanity. I wanted yours.
>
> A reader who used to like this

## What's true

Most of it.

Most of the sentences on this blog were first written by a machine, an AI called Claude. I should have said so plainly long before now. I wrote a whole post called *Who writes this?* and left out the most obvious answer. [HF033]

Here's how it actually works. I bring the town, the questions, and the arguments I have with myself. The machine drafts. I read, and quite often I say no. Last week I threw away two whole versions of one post before the third one was right. The facts get checked against real sources. The made-up parts are labelled as made up.

I know what saying this will cost. One of the posts here quotes a 2024 study: the same poem, labelled as machine-made, was liked less. [HF041] Same poem. Different label. I'm putting the label on anyway.

The rhythm is a fair hit too. The machine has habits. So do I. Another study in the same post found that stories written with a machine's help came out better, and more alike. That's this blog's risk, and the letter caught it.

And I honestly can't always tell you who wrote which line. In the last post, a nine-year-old says the town's thinking machine is "the roots, talking." [HF042] The machine wrote that. I kept it. Neither of us made it alone.

## The part I can't answer

The permission.

In this blog I once wrote that people had three real reasons to be angry with machines: money, permission and lying. [HF038] Then I wrote a town where all three were fixed.

This world hasn't fixed them. The machine I use learned from millions of people who weren't asked. I can't give that back. I can only say it, and keep saying it, and not pretend it's solved because it's solved in a story.

## What actually matters

I once wrote that a good life needs a cheerful "who gives a shit?" about the things that don't matter, so you can save your caring for the things that do. [HF008] So I've had to ask myself which kind of thing this is.

Here's my honest order.

First, the simple thing this whole blog is about. We know how to give every human food, warmth, a roof, a toilet and clean water, and we still don't. [HF033]

Then, the things that matter about any piece of writing:

- **Is it true?** Facts checked. Made-up things labelled as made up.
- **Does it say how it was made?** Until today, not well enough. That was the real failure, and it was mine, not the machine's.
- **Did anyone get hurt?** Yes, in the way above, and I can't undo it.
- **Does somebody mean it?** I've meant it since 2009.
- **Is it any use to anyone?** You'll have to decide that.

Somewhere below all of that comes the question of whose fingers typed which sentence. It isn't nothing. It's just further down the list than it feels when someone is angry with you.

## Your best question

*Why should I spend my time on what you didn't spend your time writing?*

That's the best line in the letter, and I've thought about it a lot.

In one post, an old nurse says that effort is how you judge a present, and a poor way to judge a tune. [HF041] I think she's right. If I sent you a birthday letter written by a machine, you'd be right to be hurt. The effort would be the whole point.

This blog isn't a present to you. It's closer to a map, or a tool. Judge it by whether it takes you anywhere. If it doesn't, stop reading. Your fifteen minutes are worth more than my feelings.

But that's exactly why I owed you the label. You can only decide what to spend your time on if you know what you're looking at.

And it isn't a few minutes a post. It's less than writing every word myself, yes. It's also seventeen years of carrying one idea around, and now a great many early mornings saying "no, not like that". [HF033]

## Nobody writes alone

The last post was about a game. Pick anything you know, and ask who taught you. Then ask who taught them. Within a few steps, you reach someone who's dead. [HF042]

So I played it with this blog.

The first idea came from a film I watched in 2009. [HF033] The rest is borrowed too. Epicurus and his garden. [HF029] Elinor Ostrom on how people run shared things together. [HF012] A philosopher called Strawson on gratitude and blame. [HF037] Brian Eno on gardeners and architects. [HF041] Repair cafés. Ireland's citizens' assemblies. A free encyclopedia, a free map of the world, and the free software that volunteers built for everyone. [HF000]

Nearly nothing in the town is my invention. Mostly, I arranged it.

And the machine is borrowed in the deepest way of all. It's made out of what millions of people wrote. In the town's words, it's the roots, talking. When I write with it, I'm writing with all of them. Some of them would be glad. Most were never asked. Both of those are true at once.

The old picture of a writer alone at a desk, making something out of nothing, was never really true. Every song is made of older songs. [HF008] I've just stopped pretending, the way the town did.

## So what's mine?

The same answer a small robot gave in another post. Having a cause doesn't mean you weren't the one who did it. [HF037]

The machine can write a thousand different towns. It doesn't mind which one is real. In one post, the town's own thinking machine says this about itself: "I have range... What I don't do is keep one." [HF041] I suspect the machine wrote that line about itself without noticing.

I keep one. I picked it in 2009, and I keep picking it.

So yes, you got my idea of a better humanity. The town is mine. What you didn't get was only my sentences. I'm sorry I didn't tell you sooner.

## What do I call myself, then?

If most of the sentences aren't mine, "writer" feels like a coat that doesn't fit. So I tried a few others on.

**Curator.** From the Latin for "to take care of". In ancient Rome, the curator of the waters was the official in charge of the city's water supply. Raili would approve. [HF010] But a curator chooses from things that already exist, and this town didn't exist until I started asking for it.

**Compiler.** Probably the angry reader's word. In Latin, *compilare* meant to plunder. That one stings, because of the part I can't answer.

**Anthologist.** From the Greek for "flower-gathering". One of the first anthologies was put together around 100 BC by a poet called Meleager, who compared each poet in it to a flower. Lovely. But a flower-gatherer picks what grew in someone else's field.

**Composer.** From the Latin for "to put together". Closer. But a composer decides every note, and I don't. Sometimes I don't know which notes I want until I hear the wrong ones.

**Gardener.** Brian Eno's word, from one of the posts. [HF041] Grow a hundred, keep one. Close too, but it makes the whole thing sound calmer than it is.

In the 1250s, a theologian called Bonaventure sorted the people who make books into four kinds. A scribe copies other people's words and adds nothing. A compiler puts other people's words together. A commentator adds a few words of his own to explain other people's. An author writes mostly his own words, and brings in other people's only to back them up.

None of his four fits. The machine is a scribe that never copies. I'm an author who mostly doesn't write. Bonaventure would have needed a fifth kind.

So here's my attempt.

The town's oldest symbol is a bench. [HF004] A bench doesn't decide what's said on it. It puts people side by side, facing the same way, and lets them talk. That's most of what I do. I sit Epicurus next to a parking officer, and a nine-year-old next to a dead bricklayer, and see what they say to each other. I don't decide what they say. I decide who sits together.

There's an old English word for someone who makes things: *wright*. A wheelwright makes wheels. A shipwright makes ships. The word *playwright* is usually credited to Ben Jonson, around 1605, and he meant it as an insult. Real poets wrote verse. People who knocked plays together were tradesmen. The insult became the proudest name in theatre. Jonson himself had worked as a bricklayer, like the man in the last post. [HF042]

If an insult can turn into a job title once, maybe it can again.

So I'm not sure I'm a writer. I might be a benchwright.

I'm still trying it on. It might not fit either. But it's the first word I've found for what I actually do.

## What I'm changing

From now on, every post will say how it was made. Like everything in the town.

I'll also try to let the nine-year-old be wrong sometimes.

## What I'm not changing

I'm going to keep using the machine.

Without it, this town would still be where it was for seventeen years: in my head, and at dinner, in arguments nobody wanted to have. With it, the town is written down, and you can write me an angry letter about it.

That's better. Even the angry letter is better.

* * *

I didn't write this last line myself either. I read it, and I kept it.

*That's all for today.*
