# HF024: What Makes a Life Worth Living?

> Published on Nov 11, 2026

[Music for reading this post, if you want (middle-click opens in new tab)](https://music.youtube.com/watch?v=FGIgsG2im4U)

Kevin had started with a question about work.

He now had questions about play, boredom, curiosity, satisfaction, responsibility and the design of everyday experiences.

But one question remained.

If humans no longer needed to organise most of their lives around earning a living, what would they actually want to do?

More importantly, what would make a life good?

Kevin decided that this required a new investigation.

He called it:

**The Good Life Problem.**

## Is entertainment enough?

"Would humans need more entertainment if they didn't have to work?" Kevin asked.

"Why do you assume they need more entertainment?" asked Aino.

"Because entertainment is enjoyable."

"Sometimes. But perhaps people don't always want to be entertained."

Kevin looked confused.

Aino explained that entertainment can be wonderful. A film, a concert, a game, a story or a funny video can provide pleasure, excitement, emotion and rest.

There is nothing wrong with enjoying entertainment.

But perhaps entertainment is not the same as having a life that is enjoyable.

Someone might spend the entire day watching fascinating things other people have created, while wishing they had more opportunities to create something themselves.

Another person might spend hours playing a game, not because they want to escape life, but because the game offers a kind of challenge and discovery that they genuinely enjoy.

And someone else might simply want to sit in the sun and watch the world go by.

Perhaps the question is not whether humans need entertainment.

It is whether they have enough opportunities to experience ordinary life as something worth enjoying.

## What should a human life contain?

Kevin opened a new list.

"Should a good life include curiosity, challenge, dancing, fun, friendship, creation, rest, adventure and contemplation?"

"That sounds like a rather demanding checklist," said Aino.

"But are these things not important?"

"They can be. But do they all need to be present in equal amounts?"

Kevin had assumed that a good life would require balance.

A little creativity. A little physical activity. A little intellectual challenge. A little social interaction. A little rest. A little entertainment.

Perhaps a person should distribute their time evenly across all the important dimensions of life.

Aino challenged this idea.

"Imagine someone who loves mathematics. They spend most of their time exploring mathematical ideas. They have friends, enjoy good food, take walks and rest, but mathematics is clearly the centre of their life. Is that a bad balance?"

"Not necessarily."

"Imagine someone who spends most of their time gardening. Another person spends much of their life making music. Someone else loves travelling. Someone might prefer a quiet life with a small circle of friends and very little novelty."

Kevin considered this.

"So perhaps there is no universal ideal proportion."

"Exactly."

A person does not necessarily need to experience every possible kind of activity to have a fulfilling life.

And a person who devotes a great deal of time to one particular interest is not automatically living an unbalanced life.

Perhaps balance should not mean equal quantities of everything.

It might mean having a life in which the things that matter to you have room to exist.

## Can there be too much of a good thing?

Kevin was still interested in balance.

"Surely there must be a point where too much of one thing becomes a problem?"

"Sometimes," said Aino. "But we should be careful about deciding that point for other people."

A person might spend years studying a difficult subject and find it deeply fulfilling. Another might spend most of their time with family. Someone might dedicate themselves to a creative project for a long period.

From the outside, these lives might look narrow.

But the people living them might not feel that anything important is missing.

Perhaps the question is not whether someone spends too much time on one activity.

It is whether that activity prevents them from having other experiences that they themselves value.

There is a difference between being devoted to something and being trapped by it.

There is also a difference between choosing a narrow focus and having one's possibilities narrowed by circumstances.

A good society should make many different ways of living possible. It should not prescribe one ideal lifestyle and then measure everyone against it.

## The value of challenge

Kevin wondered whether a good future would be one in which everything was easy.

"Wouldn't humans be happier if they no longer had to struggle?"

"Which struggles?" asked Aino.

Kevin thought about it.

There are struggles that people do not choose: unnecessary poverty, avoidable illness, dangerous living conditions, pointless bureaucracy, exhausting repetitive tasks and the constant fear of not having enough.

Removing those burdens could make life much better.

But there are other difficulties that people actively seek.

They climb mountains, learn complicated instruments, solve mathematical problems, build things, explore unfamiliar places and attempt things they might fail at.

They choose challenges because the effort itself can be rewarding.

Perhaps humans do not need a life without difficulty.

They need more freedom to choose which difficulties are worth experiencing.

There is a difference between being forced to struggle and choosing to undertake a difficult challenge.

And there is a difference between removing unnecessary suffering and removing every opportunity for effort, growth or discovery.

A future without compulsory hardship need not be a future without ambition.

## Curiosity and the unknown

Aino suggested that curiosity might be one of the most important ingredients.

Curiosity gives people a reason to explore something they do not yet understand.

It can lead to science, art, travel, conversation, play, invention and countless small discoveries.

Sometimes curiosity produces something useful. Sometimes it does not.

A person might spend an afternoon investigating why a particular bird behaves strangely. They might learn to make a type of bread simply because they are curious about the process. They might take apart an old machine without knowing whether they will ever use what they discover.

Not every exploration needs a practical outcome.

Perhaps one of the most valuable freedoms in a future society would be the freedom to follow a question without first needing to justify why the answer matters.

Kevin noted this.

"Would that not produce a lot of wasted effort?"

"Probably," said Aino.

"That seems inefficient."

"Some of the most interesting discoveries begin as things that look inefficient."

## Not everything needs to be enjoyable

Kevin was beginning to think that a good society should make everything pleasant.

Aino disagreed.

"Would you expect someone to enjoy grieving when a friend dies?"

"No."

"Would you expect someone to enjoy admitting that they have hurt another person?"

"Not necessarily."

"What about supporting someone who is ill, learning something frustratingly difficult or facing an uncomfortable truth?"

Kevin paused.

Perhaps some experiences are painful but still important.

Some are meaningful precisely because we care about what is happening.

A person may find it difficult to support a friend through a crisis, but would not want to avoid doing so. Someone might feel anxious about a challenging conversation, but know that it is necessary.

There are several different things that can make an experience valuable.

Immediate pleasure is one.

But so are long-term satisfaction, meaning, connection, emotional richness, personal growth and the feeling that something matters.

These qualities do not always arrive together.

A good life cannot simply be a sequence of pleasant moments.

Nor should it be a sequence of obligations and sacrifices.

Perhaps it should contain room for the full range of human experience, including experiences that are difficult, sad, uncomfortable or uncertain.

## The danger of compulsory happiness

Kevin raised another possibility.

"What if a future society became so good at providing opportunities for enjoyable experiences that everyone was expected to be happy?"

Aino laughed.

"That sounds exhausting."

Imagine a society where everyone is encouraged to discover their passion, develop their potential, maintain a healthy social life, stay curious, pursue meaningful challenges and make the most of every day.

It sounds positive.

Until someone simply wants to do very little.

Perhaps they have had a long week. Perhaps they are not interested in discovering a new passion. Perhaps they want to sit on a bench, watch the clouds and have a rather mediocre afternoon.

Would that be considered a failure?

A society can accidentally turn even freedom into an obligation.

Happiness can become another performance. Self-development can become another form of pressure. People can begin to feel that they must optimise their lives just as earlier societies expected them to optimise their productivity.

Perhaps people should also have the freedom to have a rather mediocre Tuesday.

Kevin thought this deserved to be recorded.

## A life without a timetable?

Kevin then asked whether humans would still organise their lives into working hours and leisure time.

"Wouldn't they need a schedule?"

"Some people would want one," said Aino. "Others might prefer something more flexible."

People might organise their days around curiosity, relationships, seasons, shared projects, physical needs and changing interests.

Someone might spend a month learning to build a boat, then lose interest and begin studying astronomy.

Another might follow a regular daily routine because they find it comforting.

A group might decide to meet every Wednesday to play music. A person might spend the morning helping a neighbour and the afternoon doing absolutely nothing.

Plans would still be useful. Commitments would still matter. Some activities would need coordination.

But the calendar would not necessarily divide human life into one period for earning the right to live and another for actually living.

Perhaps time would become something people organise around the lives they want to have.

Not the other way around.

## Does a good life need everything?

Kevin returned to his original list.

Curiosity. Challenge. Dancing. Fun. Friendship. Creation. Rest. Adventure. Contemplation. Care. Discovery.

"Should all these things be available to everyone?"

"That is a better question," said Aino.

Perhaps a good society should make a wide range of experiences possible, without requiring everyone to pursue them.

People should be able to explore, create, learn, rest, connect, play, care for others, take on difficult challenges or devote themselves to a particular interest.

They should have access to the tools, spaces, knowledge and resources that make these activities possible.

They should be able to change direction without feeling that they have wasted their lives.

They should be able to pursue something deeply without being expected to pursue everything.

And they should be able to enjoy simple pleasures without having to turn them into achievements.

The important thing might not be that every person experiences every aspect of life.

It might be that no one is prevented from exploring the aspects that matter to them.

## The freedom to find out

Kevin looked at the list again.

He had started by asking what humans would do without work.

Now he wondered whether that had been the wrong question.

Perhaps the future was not about finding enough activities to fill the time left over after work.

Perhaps it was about creating conditions in which people could discover what they actually wanted to do.

Some would build things. Some would study. Some would dance. Some would explore. Some would care for others. Some would create beautiful objects. Some would spend years on a single difficult problem.

Some would do a little of everything.

Some would do very little.

And people would change.

Aino looked at Kevin's document.

"Have you found the ideal balance?"

"No," said Kevin.

"Good."

"Why is that good?"

"Because perhaps there isn't one."

Kevin considered this.

"Then what should society optimise?"

Aino thought for a while.

"Perhaps it should stop trying to optimise people's lives."

Kevin was confused.

"That sounds inefficient."

"Perhaps that's the point."

Maybe the aim of a good future is not to maximise productivity, entertainment, happiness or even personal development.

Maybe it is to create the freedom and conditions in which different people can discover what makes their own lives worth living.

Not one recipe for the good life.

Not one approved balance of work, play, rest and challenge.

Just a world with enough possibility for people to find their own way.
