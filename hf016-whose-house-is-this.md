# HF016: Whose house is this?

> Published on Nov 9, 2026

Leena moved in next door last week. It's her sixty-fourth home.

Before this, she lived in a penthouse in Barcelona, with the whole Mediterranean in the window. Now she lives in a small studio above the bike repair shop in our suburb, which is, let's be honest, somewhere near nowhere.

She's thrilled. Exactly as thrilled as she was about the penthouse.

"I'd had enough sea," she said. "I wanted a forest, a library, and snow."

## How moving works

Nobody owns a home. You live in one for as long as you like, and then you don't.

The Earth-monitor shows what's free now, what's free soon, and who'd like to swap. You pick a place and you go. Some people stay forty years. Some move every season. Most do a bit of both over a lifetime.

You travel light, too. A bag or two, some clothes, a few keepsakes. Everything else, the house already has.

And when you leave, you leave a note for whoever comes next. Leena's studio came with one:

> The radiator clanks at 3 a.m. It's friendly. The baker downstairs gives away yesterday's bread at 7. Say hi to the cat on the stairs, she isn't mine either. — someone

## Why a penthouse isn't better than a studio

In the old world, a penthouse with a sea view meant something. Money, success, being someone. A studio in the suburbs meant something too, usually the opposite.

Here, nobody pays for anything, so a home doesn't say anything about you. The penthouse just means lots of stairs, a big view and a lot of wind. The studio means cosy, close to the bakery, and a clanking radiator. Places aren't better or worse anymore. They're just different.

And "better" turns personal. It depends on what you're in the mood for right now: sea or forest, noise or quiet, a big city or a small library with a pink bench outside.

Doesn't everyone want the penthouse? Some do, for a while. Popular places have waiting lists, and you can sign up and live somewhere else in the meantime. But after so many moves, most people care more about *new* than about *best*. The excitement is in the change itself.

## A few bad places, on purpose

Nearly every home is in good shape. Things are built to last, and fixing them is somebody's hobby.

But a few places are kept rough on purpose, and people love them for it. A draughty fisherman's hut with an outdoor toilet. A lighthouse with three hundred stairs and no lift. And one old-world rental flat preserved exactly as it was: damp corner, broken intercom, and a framed collection of the landlord's angry notes.

Nobody has to live in them. That's exactly why people want to. It's like camping, but with history.

## Hundreds of years of moving

People have lived like this for a long time now. It started a few generations after what the history books call the Great Shrug: the moment people measurably started to walk away from buying things. (More about that another time.) [[HF018]](https://humanityfiction.mataroa.blog/blog/hf018-the-great-shrug)

The old world took a long time to fade. Money only finally disappeared within Aino's lifetime. But the habit of moving around is much older than anyone alive.

Over the centuries, people have lived in so many kinds of places that every new one feels like a small adventure. Treehouses, houseboats, old castles, concrete towers, cabins with no roads at all.

And sometimes people go back. Leena has lived in the same tiny flat in Lisbon three times. "It has the best morning light in the world," she says. "I keep forgetting, so I keep going back to check."

* * *

Leena asked how many homes I've had. Twenty-three. And how long I've been here: two years, my longest ever.

"Why stay?" she asked.

I thought about it. The library. The drain crew. Aino. Eevi and her wallet full of leaves.

"Probably the bench," I said.
