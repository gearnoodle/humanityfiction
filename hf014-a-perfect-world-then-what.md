# HF014: A perfect world. Then what?

> Published on Nov 1, 2026

One Tuesday, our town's Earth-monitor was empty. Every issue closed. Every drain flowing, every bench steady, every courgette eaten.

People cheered. By Thursday, they were restless.

Someone loosened a bench screw just to have something to fix. Eevi invented a game where you hide a bench and everyone has to find it. And someone proposed digging a canal through town, for no reason at all, "for the joy of it."

## Why perfect gets boring

**We get used to everything.** Psychologists call it the hedonic treadmill. After a big change, good or bad, most people drift back to their usual level of happiness. A perfect world would feel normal within a year.

**We'd rather do than just feel.** The philosopher Robert Nozick imagined a machine that could give you any experience you wanted, forever. Perfect happiness, plugged in. Most people say they wouldn't use it. We don't just want to feel good. We want to actually do things.

**We invent obstacles for fun.** Another philosopher, Bernard Suits, said playing a game is choosing to overcome obstacles you don't need to overcome. Golf is just trying to get a ball into a hole the hard way. Give people a perfect world, and they'll build mountains to climb in it.

So nobody here really wants a perfect world. What they want is a **perfect floor, and an open ceiling**. The floor: nobody suffers for no reason, nobody is trapped, nobody goes hungry. The ceiling: everything else, open forever. Play, mistakes, progress, canals.

## The one thing you can't share

Almost everything in this world got solved by sharing. Food, homes, medicine, songs. Copy it, fork it, pass it on.

Relationships don't work that way. A friendship exists only between two particular people. You can't copy my friendship with Aino and give it to someone lonely.

So once everything else is abundant, relationships become the main thing that's still scarce. Together with time, they're most of the open ceiling.

## What changed, and what didn't

The old world bent relationships out of shape. Marriages held together by money. Friendships that were really networking. Most waking hours spent with coworkers nobody chose. People too tired after work to call anyone.

Aristotle said there are three kinds of friendship: friends who are useful to you, friends who are fun, and friends you love for who they are. The old world ran heavily on the first kind. Take away money and careers, and most "useful" friendships simply evaporate. What's left are the two kinds that matter more.

There's also time. One study estimated it takes about 50 hours together to become casual friends, and over 200 hours to become close friends. In a world of three-hour working months, everyone can afford the 200 hours.

But some things stay unequal. Shyness, charm, temperament, and what we learned as children about whether closeness is safe. Some people will be surrounded by friends. Some will be alone.

And here's the hard part. In the old world, loneliness could hide behind "I'm too busy." Here, nobody is too busy. Every relationship is a free choice, every day, on both sides.

## Someone good to be near

"Gathering the best people around you" sounds a bit like collecting. Treating people as useful assets is an old-world habit.

The philosopher Martin Buber described two ways of meeting someone. In the first, they're a thing to you: a customer, a contact, a means to something. In the second, you meet them as a whole person, fully present. The old world was built on the first kind. This world makes room for the second, but it can't force it.

So maybe the better question isn't "will everyone find the best people?" It's "will everyone become someone good to be near?"

The skills of the light ones help: noticing feelings without obeying them, saying "I'm hurt" instead of using it as a weapon, forgiving quickly, being curious instead of offended. Those aren't just personal habits. They're what good relationships are made of.

## The hard-people hobby

Some people who are good with people go looking for a challenge. Like Raili with her sewers, they're drawn to the difficult cases.

Marja collects grumpy people. Her current project is Pekka, the grumpiest man in town. For thirty years in the old world, he was a parking enforcement officer. Everyone hated him, and he got used to it. He has scared off every friendly neighbour for two decades.

Marja has spent about 140 hours with him. "Sixty to go," she says.

Why bother? "Easy people are lovely," she told me. "But difficult people are where you learn the most. And they're usually difficult because something hurt them once."

She's careful about one thing. "If I treat him like a mountain to climb, he'll know. You have to actually like him." And Pekka is allowed to say no. Friendship can't be forced, not even kindly. If he really wants to be left alone, she'll stop.

Some people climb mountains. Some people befriend Pekka.

## What a design can't do

No design can make people love each other. It can only make it easy to meet: benches, drain crews, armies, shared tasks where people work side by side. It can make it normal to ask for company. And it can have a Loneliness Army for the rest.

Every other kind of abundance in this world was built. Love can't be. It can only be offered, again and again, by ordinary people who don't have to.

Maybe that's what keeps a perfect world human.

* * *

Yesterday, Pekka sat down next to Marja on the pink bench. He didn't say a word.

She counted it as an hour.
