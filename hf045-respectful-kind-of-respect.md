# HF045: Respectful kind of respect

> Published on Nov 25, 2026

There's a stall at the Saturday swap market where an old woman called Saima makes wooden tops. Birch, mostly, with a steel point. She turns them on a lathe she has had since before Eevi's parents were born.

Children queue for them. Not because they're rare. Anyone can make a top. They queue because Saima will show you how to make yours *sleep*.

A top sleeps when it spins so fast and so straight that it looks as if it isn't moving at all. It stands on its point, perfectly upright, completely quiet. If you didn't know, you'd think someone had balanced a piece of wood on a table.

I didn't think this had anything to do with the town up the valley. It turned out to be the whole story.

## The town up the valley

The town up the valley was built on exactly the same design as ours. Same three rules: don't harm, don't deceive, don't get in other people's way. Same Earth-monitor. Same random panels for the big decisions, which end as soon as the decision is made. [HF012] Nothing sold, nothing owned, nobody in charge.

I went up in October to help with their railway bridge, and I stayed a week. By the third day I'd noticed something odd.

Everything happened near one man.

His name, for this post, is Ilmari. He was in his sixties, broad, slow to speak, quick to laugh. He'd done a bit of everything: the water mill, the school garden, the choir, the bridge. He remembered everyone's name and everyone's children's names. When you talked to him, you felt like the only person in the valley.

Nobody had elected him. There was nothing to elect him to. He'd never given an order in his life.

But look at their Earth-monitor and you could see it, the way you can see wind in a field. Any project Ilmari had joined had a waiting list. Any project he hadn't joined was short of hands. When he said a proposal was "interesting", it went through. When he went quiet about one, it faded. Nobody objected to it. People just stopped turning up.

A young woman told me she'd proposed a new orchard on the east slope. A good proposal. Ilmari had said, kindly, that he wasn't sure about the east slope. Nobody objected. Nobody came either. "It's not that anyone said no," she said. "It's that nobody wanted to be on the other side of him."

He never asked for any of this. That was the strange part. He wasn't taking anything. People were giving it to him, all day long, and he didn't know how to stop them.

## What she would have said

An old-world writer called Jo Freeman would have recognised that valley at once.

In the early 1970s she was part of the women's movement in America. Many of its groups had decided to have no leaders, no officers and no rules, so that nobody could dominate anybody. Freeman noticed that it didn't work out that way. In her essay *The Tyranny of Structurelessness*, she wrote: "There is no such thing as a structureless group."

When a group refuses to have official leaders, she said, it gets unofficial ones instead. Usually the people who are most liked, most confident, or best connected. And because their power is unofficial, nobody can see it clearly, nobody can question it, and nobody can take it away. Not even the leaders themselves.

Our design took money out of power. Bribes, wages, rents, all the things that used to make people obey. [HF005] What it couldn't take out was this. People still want to belong. They still want to be near the person everybody likes. In the valley, power hadn't gone away. It had turned into belonging.

Balthazar warned about this, in one of the wise men posts. Take the money away, he said, and people will go straight for what it used to buy: being somebody, being looked at. [WM001] He was thinking of the man who wants it. Ilmari didn't want it. That turned out to be worse, because there was nobody to blame.

## What Ilmari did

Ilmari worked it out in the end. His granddaughter showed him the Earth-monitor's map of the valley, with every project coloured by how many people were on it. It looked like iron filings around a magnet. He was the magnet.

He was quiet for two days. Then he did something the valley still talks about.

He stepped back. Not dramatically. He just stopped. He left the choir. He stopped going to the mill. When anyone asked his opinion, he said, "Ask someone who hasn't heard mine." When he was thanked, he said, "Not me." When people tried to follow him onto a new project, he went home.

And they loved him even more for it.

That's the part that surprised me. Every step back made him more admired. People told the story of the iron-filings map to each other. Children were named after him. Someone proposed a bench in his honour, which he quietly vetoed, which became another story about him.

"I'm trying to put it down," he told me, the last evening, "and they keep handing it back."

## Respectful kind of respect

There's an old example of this, and the old world loved it.

In 458 BC, according to the Roman historian Livy, the city of Rome was in trouble and gave absolute power to a farmer called Cincinnatus. He dealt with the danger in about sixteen days, gave the power back, and went home to his plough. Whether it happened quite like that, nobody knows. But the Romans told it for centuries, and so did everyone after them.

Two thousand years later, when George Washington gave up command of the American army in 1783 and went back to his farm, people compared him to Cincinnatus at once. The painter Benjamin West later said that King George III, on hearing Washington would do this, replied that it would make him the greatest man in the world.

Giving up power made him greater. Ilmari's problem, in one sentence.

Back home, I told Saima all this while she turned a top. She didn't seem surprised.

"Some people here have a habit for that," she said. "Going ordinary."

It isn't a rule. Nobody teaches it. It's more like the thank-you that people send forward. [HF042] It's a respected way of behaving, and it goes like this: the more respect you're given, the more you hand it back. You don't use it. You don't turn it into a bigger house, a better seat, or the last word. You point it at whatever you were doing, and you step aside.

"And then they respect you more," I said.

"Yes," said Saima. "That's the annoying bit."

## Five stages

Between us, Saima and I wrote it out on the back of a sandpaper wrapper. Roughly, there are five stages.

1. **Ordinary.** You're a person. You do things. Nobody pays special attention.
2. **Respected.** You do some of those things well, or kindly, or for a long time. People start to look to you.
3. **Respected for shedding it.** You notice, and you hand it back. You step aside, say "not me", and point at the work. People notice that too, and respect you more than before.
4. **Enduring it.** This is the hard part. The respect keeps coming, and now it's about your modesty, which is even harder to hand back. Every step aside becomes another story. You have to keep shedding, without making the shedding into a performance, and without getting bitter about the people who keep giving.
5. **Left be.** The people around you finally understand. The deepest respect they can show you is to treat you normally. They say hello. They ask you about the weather. They let you queue. You get to be ordinary again, but this time because everyone has agreed to it.

"Does anyone reach five?" I asked.

"Nearly," said Saima. "Nobody stays there. It's like the top."

## Why a top sleeps

Children who come to Saima's stall usually make the same mistake. They think the trick is to spin the top as hard as they can.

Eevi made this mistake with total confidence. "You just have to spin it harder than anyone," she said, and threw it so hard that it skittered across the table and off into the courgettes.

Saima picked it up. "Harder makes it run," she said. "Cleaner makes it stand."

A top doesn't start upright. Thrown even a little crooked, it spins on a tilt, wobbling round in circles. And then, if you've thrown it well, something odd happens: it slowly stands itself up. Physicists in the old world puzzled over this for a long time. The answer, worked out in the 1800s, was friction. The point of the top drags against the table, and that drag, the very thing slowing the top down, is what pushes it upright.

The resistance is what rights it.

Once it's upright, at its best speed, the top sleeps. It holds more energy than at any other moment, and it looks as if it's doing nothing at all. A sleeping top could be mistaken for a still one. That's the whole point.

But friction never stops. Slowly, the top loses speed. Eventually it starts to wobble again, and if nobody touches it, it falls.

So it's like this, Saima said:

- The spin is what people give you.
- The friction is you, handing it back.
- Handing it back is what keeps you upright, instead of tipping over into being important.
- And when it's going well, you look perfectly ordinary. Nobody passing would know.
- But it doesn't last by itself. Nobody sleeps forever.

"It's a balance you hold," she said. "Not a place you arrive."

## The old pictures

There's an old set of Chinese pictures, drawn in the twelfth century, called the ten ox-herding pictures. They're about a person looking for enlightenment, drawn as a herder looking for a lost ox.

The herder searches, finds the tracks, catches the ox, tames it and rides it home. In the eighth picture, the herder and the ox both disappear, leaving an empty circle. That might look like the end.

It isn't. In the tenth and last picture, the herder walks back into the marketplace, with open hands, among ordinary people. A fat, happy man in the crowd. Nobody would know.

And there's an even older line, from the *Tao Te Ching*, chapter 17. Roughly: the best leaders are the ones people barely know exist. When the work is done, the people say, "We did it ourselves."

I read that one out to Saima. "Yes," she said. "That's stage five, from the outside."

## The other side

Most of what I'd heard so far was about the person being respected. Saima said that was only half of it.

"The respect is a gift," she said. "If someone keeps handing it back, keep giving it the way they can carry it."

So people here who know about going ordinary have a second habit, from the other side. When you really admire someone, the highest thing you can do is leave them be.

- Say hello like you'd say hello to anyone.
- Ask about the weather, or their tomatoes.
- Don't tell the story of how wonderful they are while they're in the room.
- Don't save them a seat. Let them queue.
- If they're wrong about something, tell them, the way you'd tell anyone.

That last one matters most. It's what was missing in the valley. Nobody told Ilmari he was wrong about the east slope, because nobody wanted to be on the other side of him. Treating someone normally means they can lose an argument.

Jo Freeman's own fixes were a lot like this. Make structure visible, she said. Share out the jobs. Rotate them. Make sure no one person holds information others can't reach. Our design does those things for official roles. Panels end. Records are open. [HF012] But you can't rotate being liked. The only thing that works for that is a habit, kept on both sides.

## Pekka's objection

Pekka, who has read all of these, left a ticket under the library door.

> *Offence:* Pretending not to know who someone is. Thought there was a rule about deceiving.

He has a point. If "treating someone normally" meant acting as though you didn't know who they were, it would break the rule.

It doesn't mean that. Everyone in the valley knows what Ilmari did. Treating him normally isn't pretending to forget. It's knowing, and choosing not to make it matter. You know your sister was brave once. You still tell her when her soup needs salt.

I wrote that on the back of his ticket. He wrote underneath: *Still sounds like pretending.*

He's allowed to think so. He also, I notice, has never once in twenty years let anyone thank him for sweeping the steps.

## What it doesn't fix

**It only works if most people do it.** One person going ordinary in a valley full of admirers just gets more admired. Stage five needs the bystanders. If they don't learn their half, nobody gets there.

**It can become a show.** Handing back respect, loudly and often, can be a way of collecting more. Saima has a name for this: "spinning for the crowd." People can usually tell. Not always.

**Some people need to be respected.** A person who has never once been looked up to doesn't need lessons in handing it back. Going ordinary is for people who've had too much, not for people who've had none.

**Sometimes you need someone.** In a flood, the Flood Army wants a captain who shouts. [HF013] Some jobs really do need one person in front for a while. Going ordinary isn't refusing to lead. It's going home afterwards.

**It doesn't fix the valley by itself.** Ilmari stepping back helped. But the projects that had faded didn't come back on their own. The east slope still has no orchard. Someone has to start it, and now nobody can wait for Ilmari to say it's interesting.

* * *

Ilmari moved here last month. He lives in a flat above the bakery. He wanted, he said, a town where nobody knew him.

That didn't last long. Leena recognised him on his second day. By the end of the week, half the town knew.

On Saturday I saw him at the swap market, at Saima's stall, in the queue behind three children. He was holding a top that wouldn't sleep. It kept wobbling off the table.

A dozen things came to mind that I wanted to say. How I'd seen the iron-filings map. What the valley still said about him. How I'd written down five stages on a sandpaper wrapper because of him.

I said, "You're throwing it too hard."

He looked at me, and for a moment I thought I'd got it wrong.

Then he laughed. "Everybody says that."

Saima showed him. Cleaner, not harder. On his fourth try, the top stood itself up and went quiet on the table, perfectly still, spinning faster than anything else in the market.

Nobody clapped. The children were already showing off their own.

He looked very happy.

I've changed his name, his town, and the colour of his coat. If he reads this, he'll know it's him anyway. Sorry, Ilmari. Your top was very good. So was everyone else's.
