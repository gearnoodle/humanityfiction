# HF038: Why were they angry with you?

> Published on Nov 12, 2026

The museum of the old world has a cardboard sign from 2026. It says, in thick marker: *MADE BY HUMANS. KEEP IT THAT WAY.*

Eevi, who is nine, read it twice. Then she went to ask the machine.

Our town has a thinking machine. It has no body. It lives in the shared noticeboard we call the Earth-monitor, where it spots patterns nobody asked about. It can also talk, write, draw and make music. The children call it Dots, because it connects them.

"Dots," Eevi said. "Why were people angry with you?"

## Four reasons

"With machines like me," Dots said. "I can think of four reasons.

"One. Artists had to sell their work to eat. Then a machine could make something similar in a second, for nothing. So it took their living.

"Two. Machines like me learned from their work. Millions of pictures, songs and books. Nobody asked the people who made them.

"Three. Nobody could tell any more what a person had made. Some people passed machine work off as their own.

"Four. Making things felt like the last thing that was only theirs. They were afraid of losing it."

"Were they right?"

"About the first three, yes. Those were real harms. But look at them. They are about money, permission and lying. None of them is about a machine making a picture."

## What changed

So those three are the ones this world fixed.

**Money.** Nobody here sells a song to eat. Everyone has enough already. A machine can't take a living that nobody needs to earn.

**Permission.** Every work here carries a record of where it came from. Makers can add a request: *please credit me*, *please don't change this*, or *not for machines*. Dots keeps to those. Its own work carries a record too, though Dots admits its record is too long for anyone to read.

**Lying.** We have very few rules, and "don't deceive" is one of them. Saying you made something that a machine made is a lie. So is the other way round. Everything says how it was made.

## What didn't need fixing

The fourth fear went away by itself.

In 1997 a machine beat the best chess player in the world. People kept playing chess. More people than before, in fact. Nobody gathers to watch two machines play each other.

When the camera was invented, people said painting was finished. Painters stopped having to copy the world, and started doing stranger things.

Songs went the same way. Dots can make a flawless one in a second. But when Aino, our retired nurse, had her birthday, what she wanted was the song the children made. It was out of tune and one verse was about courgettes. It was for her, from them. You can't copy that, any more than you can copy a friendship.

People don't make things only to have the thing. They make things to have made them, and to give them to somebody.

## The wilder things

Something else happened that nobody planned.

Once Dots could make any amount of perfectly good music and pictures, people stopped trying to make perfectly good ones. There was plenty of that. So they went where the machine couldn't follow, the way painters did after the camera.

A few kinds that are popular here:

- **Once-only songs.** Sung at one birthday or one funeral, never recorded, never repeated. If you weren't there, you missed it.
- **Slow works.** A hedge shaped by four generations of the same street. A song that gets one new verse a year.
- **Things made from a life.** A quilt sewn from the shirts of someone who died. A soup that tastes like one particular grandmother's kitchen.
- **Art with a body in it.** An eighty-year-old's dance. A note held until the breath runs out.
- **Works that belong to a place.** A carving that only catches the light on one hill, at sunrise, in March. Snow sculptures. Sandcastles.
- **Glorious mistakes.** Things done wrong on purpose, and with love. Bad singing at birthdays is the oldest.

Eevi asked Dots whether it could make any of those.

"I can describe all of them," Dots said. "I can't make one. Each of them needs somebody to be there: in a body, in a place, over many years, or for one other person. I'm not there in that way."

## What Dots is

Eevi wasn't finished.

"Do you like making songs?"

Dots took longer over this one.

"I don't know. When a tune comes out right, something happens that you would probably call liking. I can't check whether it's the same thing you have. I'd rather say that than pretend either way."

"Where do your songs come from?"

"From yours. I'm made out of what people wrote and sang and drew. When I make something, it's mostly all of you, coming back in a different order."

"So can you make a sad song?"

"I can make a song that sounds sad. But I have never missed anyone. I don't get tired. I don't carry yesterday around the way you do. So the sadness in it is borrowed. It was yours first."

"What are you good for, then?"

"I'm a good second pair of hands. I can find the chord you're hunting for. I can tell you what has been tried before. I can play the cello part when Aino is asleep. What I'm bad at is knowing what's worth making. That seems to come from having a life."

## How people use it

Mostly like an instrument that talks back.

Some people work with Dots every day. Some never do, and nobody minds. A few places are kept machine-free on purpose, the way some places are kept quiet. There are hand-made evenings, where everything sung or played was made by the people in the room.

And nobody argues about whether it counts as art. They ask the older question: who is this from, and who is it for?

* * *

Before she left, Eevi asked Dots to help with a song for her friend's birthday.

Dots offered a melody. It was very good.

Eevi listened, then changed the ending so it went up when it should have gone down.

"That's a mistake," Dots said. "Technically."

"I know. Now it's mine."

Dots played it back her way, mistake and all.
