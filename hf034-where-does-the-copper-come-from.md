# HF034: Where does the copper come from?

> Published on Nov 11, 2026

Eevi has decided to build the machine from the unfinished wall. She still doesn't know what it does. She does know it needs a copper pipe.

So we went to the materials library, which is the shed behind the actual library and about four times its size. Heikki was at the desk. After thirty years in a warehouse he still likes knowing where everything is, and here, at last, nobody shouts at him about it.

"Where does copper come from?" Eevi asked.

"Shelf nine."

"Before shelf nine."

Heikki looked it up.

"And before the mountain?"

"A star, apparently. We don't have the paperwork."

## Most of it is already dug up

The old world spent centuries pulling metal out of the ground. Then it used most things briefly and buried them again, somewhere else, mixed with yoghurt pots.

It knew better. Making aluminium from old cans took about a twentieth of the energy of making it from ore. A tonne of old phones held far more gold than a tonne of rock from a gold mine. The medals at the 2020 Tokyo Olympics were made from metal recovered from millions of donated phones and gadgets.

So most of our mining happens above ground. The copper is already here. It's just currently being a kettle.

## Everything knows what it's made of

Every object carries a small record, like the songs do: what it's made of, where that came from, and how to get it out again.

It's a family tree for atoms. Eevi's pipe has had four lives that we know of. Nothing in the record says who owned it, only what it was.

## Made to come apart

In the 1920s the big lightbulb makers quietly agreed that no bulb should last more than a thousand hours. Longer-lasting bulbs were bad for business. Eevi made me repeat that one, too.

Here things are built the other way round: to last, and then to come apart. Screws, not glue. One kind of metal where three would be clever. If something can't be taken apart, that's treated as a mistake in the design.

Taking things apart is somebody's hobby, naturally. The unmakers have their own crew, their own waitlists, and strong opinions about glue.

## Borrowed, not owned

Nobody owns copper. You have some for a while.

The materials library works like the other one. You take what the job needs. When the thing you made is finished with, the material comes back. The Earth-monitor shows what's on the shelves in every town, so a shortage in one place is a delivery from another.

Leftovers travel too. An old-world town in Denmark called Kalundborg had factories passing each other their waste heat and by-products for decades, because it was cheaper. Here the smelter's spare heat warms the greenhouse and, on Thursdays, the sauna.

## When we really do have to dig

Nothing goes round perfectly. A little is lost each time: worn off, rusted, scattered too thin to collect. So some digging continues.

But a mine is about the hardest thing there is to undo. By the two questions, how many people does it touch and how hard is it to undo, it gets the slowest and widest process we have. Many towns, a panel chosen by lottery, a small trial first. Someone speaks for the mountain, and someone for the people who aren't born yet. The people who live nearest aren't outvoted. If it's bad for them, it finds another way.

And who goes down? In the old world it was the same answer as the drains: whoever had the fewest other options. Some of them were children. Here, machines go in first. Kevin has cousins.

## When there isn't enough

Sometimes there really isn't enough of something.

Then nobody bids for it. The dials decide: what is it needed for, how many people does that touch, is there another way? Medicine tends to come before gadgets. And a design that depends on a rare metal gets treated like a job nobody wants. It's a design problem.

## The old dumps

The richest mines we have are the old world's rubbish dumps. They very kindly put everything in one place.

There's an army for it, of course. They call themselves the Unburying Army, and they say it's like archaeology, only everything is from the same Tuesday.

"When you're done with it."

"What if the machine is never done?"

"Then at least somebody will know where the pipe is."
